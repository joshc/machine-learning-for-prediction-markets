    Glossary¶

    These definitions establish the book's usage. Venue-specific legal or API terminology can differ; the contract rules and relevant documentation govern actual behavior.

    Abstention. Choosing not to issue a forecast or not to trade because the evidence, information, rules, or execution conditions fail a specified requirement. It is a valid output, not a software error to be disguised.

    Adverse selection. The tendency for counterparties who trade against a quote to be better informed about value than the quote setter. A resting bid's chance of filling can increase precisely when the outcome has become less favorable to its buyer.

    Ask. A price at which a specified quantity is offered for sale. Buying requires executable offers; a displayed best ask does not promise unlimited quantity or a fill after latency.

    Available cash. Cash that is actually free to support another action after accounting for settled balances and reservations. It is not necessarily the same as account equity or the face value of collateral.

    Backtest. An explicitly specified simulation using historical observations. Its usefulness depends on which information, order states, costs, and settlement facts could genuinely have been known and acted on at each point.

    Bankroll. Capital assigned to a defined strategy or account, with its valuation convention stated. Total equity, available cash, outstanding reservations, and maximum affordable loss are different quantities; a sizing formula must specify which one its wealth variable represents.

    Base rate. The prevalence of an outcome in a specified reference population. There is no meaningful base rate without a definition of that population and the information conditioning it.

    Basis risk. Risk that an alleged hedge differs in its payoff from the position it is meant to offset. Two event contracts can differ through wording, observation times, resolution sources, cancellation policies, or collateral even if their short titles match.

    Bid. A price at which a specified quantity is sought for purchase. An immediate liquidity-taking sale crosses executable bids; a resting maker ask can instead sell if a buyer later matches it.

    Binary contract. A contract with two ordinary mutually exclusive outcomes. A simplified USD 1 YES contract pays one dollar on YES and zero on NO. Actual exceptional settlements must be handled according to the rules, not forced into this simplification.

    Brier score. The mean squared difference between a probability forecast and a binary outcome. This book uses one YES-probability error per observation, rather than a two-class sum that would double the result.

    Calibration. Agreement, in a specified population and conditioning scheme, between forecast probabilities and outcome frequencies. Finite-sample calibration estimates carry uncertainty. A constant base-rate forecast can be calibrated while providing little useful discrimination.

    Capital lockup. The period in which funds are committed to a position or settlement process and cannot be deployed elsewhere. An annualized return calculated from an optimistic holding period can misrepresent this constraint.

    Categorical event. An event with a specified set of outcomes. Sum-to-one relationships require the outcomes to be mutually exclusive and exhaustive under compatible rules; a user-interface grouping alone does not establish that property.

    Collateral. Assets securing a position's obligations. Their availability, conversion, custody, and release rules affect practical value independently of the forecast about the event.

    Confidence interval. An interval constructed by a procedure with a stated repeated-sampling coverage property under its assumptions. A computed interval is not automatically valid after strategy selection or repeated unadjusted inspection.

    Credible interval. A posterior probability interval under a specified Bayesian model and prior. It quantifies uncertainty within that model; it does not immunize the decision against misspecification.

    Decision cutoff. The latest information time admitted to a particular forecast or action. A point-in-time pipeline must enforce it for features, revised records, labels used in training, and ancillary information.

    Dependence group. A set of observations that share relevant uncertainty, such as contracts resolving on the same event or snapshots of one market. Grouping decisions should match the estimand and dependence mechanism, not merely convenient database identifiers.

    Depth. Quantities available at different order-book prices. A strategy's expected execution price depends on depth, order size, and how the book evolves before matching.

    Discrimination. The ability of a forecasting system to distinguish cases with different outcome frequencies. Ranking performance and calibration answer different questions.

    Drawdown. A decline from a prior peak in a specified wealth series. A paper equity curve marked at midpoints can understate the drawdown realizable by liquidating through available bids.

    Edge. A positive expected advantage relative to a specified feasible action and its costs, under an explicitly stated probability model. Calling an estimate an edge requires evidence beyond a favorable difference between two numbers.

    Effective sample size. A summary of how much information a dependent sample contains relative to a reference independent sample. It is model- and estimand-dependent, not automatically the number of rows or trades.

    Embargo. A withheld region around evaluation boundaries intended to reduce certain forms of temporal contamination. It does not by itself prevent shared-event or unavailable-label leakage.

    Event family. A documented group of related events, often a recurring question template or shared underlying process. It may be useful for pooling or dependency handling, but should not be treated as an arbitrary label that guarantees independence across families.

    Executable price. A price at which the chosen quantity and side could actually transact under the specified order policy. Last trades, midpoints, and a retrospective candle low are not interchangeable executable prices.

    Expected value. A probability-weighted average of possible payoffs or net outcomes. In a model-driven trade it is conditional on estimated probabilities and execution assumptions; it is not a promise about the next outcome.

    Fill. An actual or explicitly simulated match of an order quantity against a counterparty. A paper simulator must state what evidence or invented process produces its fills.

    Fill-conditioned forecast. A forecast about the outcome conditional on the order actually filling and on the information associated with that event. It can differ materially from a forecast made when the quote was posted.

    Forecast horizon. The interval between a specified forecast time and a specified outcome-related time. An event's occurrence, its public confirmation, and final settlement can have different horizons.

    Hypothesis. A proposed relationship or mechanism that has not been established by the evidence at hand. A useful research hypothesis includes a plausible alternative explanation and a procedure that could show it fails.

    Information set. The observations admitted to a probability assessment or decision. Stating an information set is central to interpreting whether a claimed improvement is new information or a restatement of what the market already knows.

    Inventory. Positions owned or owed by a trader. Inventory risk depends on event dependence and contractual payoffs, not simply on the count of tickets in an account.

    Kelly criterion. A framework for maximizing expected log wealth under specified opportunities and probabilities. Its recommended exposure is sensitive to estimation error, dependence, feasible positions, and whether its repeated-decision assumptions describe the situation.

    Label availability. The time at which an outcome label could legitimately enter training. A historical database containing the final outcome does not make that label available to a model trained before resolution.

    Leakage. Use of information in model design, fitting, evaluation, or simulated decisions that would not legitimately have been available for the stated exercise. It includes shared outcomes, revised data, tuning on the test period, and future-trained text models.

    Limit order. An instruction to trade subject to a specified limit price and other conditions. It controls acceptable price, not whether a fill will occur.

    Liquidity. The practical ability to transact a size within acceptable price and time constraints. It is not synonymous with historical trading volume or the presence of a displayed quote.

    LMSR. Logarithmic market scoring rule, a particular automated market-maker construction. Its cost function and bounded-loss properties rely on its defined mechanism; they are not universal attributes of order-book venues.

    Log loss. A proper scoring loss based on the negative logarithm of the probability assigned to the realized outcome. This book uses natural logs. Assigning zero probability to an outcome that occurs incurs infinite loss.

    Market maker. A participant or mechanism providing trading opportunities to others. A human or algorithm posting limit orders can lose to informed flow, inventory changes, fees, and operational failures even when it earns a quoted spread.

    Mark-to-market. Valuing an open position using a specified current reference price. The mark must be distinguished from cash and from what a particular position could actually be sold for.

    Multiple testing. Evaluating several hypotheses or strategy variants in a way that increases the chance of selecting an impressive result by luck. The relevant search includes informal feature, period, threshold, and reporting choices, not just a final model list.

    No-arbitrage constraint. A relationship required to exclude a specified riskless payoff opportunity under explicit trading, settlement, and financing assumptions. An observed apparent violation is only a candidate until those assumptions are checked.

    Order-book replay. A simulation driven by recorded order-book information. Its fidelity is limited by the observed message types, timestamps, missing states, participant identifiers, and effects of the simulated orders themselves.

    Paper trading. Applying a decision process to simulated balances and fills rather than funded orders. It is useful for learning and operational rehearsal, but cannot reproduce all effects of actual participation.

    Point-in-time data. Data representing what could have been known at a specified historical cutoff. Retrieval time, publication time, revisions, and label availability all matter.

    Proper scoring rule. A scoring system for which reporting one's probability distribution optimizes expected score under stated conditions. Strict propriety makes that optimum unique. This is a statement about the scoring objective, not a claim about trading profits.

    Queue priority. The ordering rules used to determine which resting orders execute. Without adequate queue information, a simulator must not assume its own order always stood at the front.

    Realized profit. Net profit recognized through a specified completed sale or settlement, after relevant costs. In the companion it is explicitly simulated unless the context says otherwise.

    Reference class. The set of cases used to estimate or interpret an outcome frequency. Changing it after seeing results can be a form of strategy selection.

    Resolution. The process that determines the contractual outcome or payout. It may include proposals, disputes, official sources, clarifications, and exceptional states, depending on the product.

    Selection bias. Distortion caused by how observations, markets, time periods, or reported results were chosen. A dataset of conveniently resolved markets can exclude important unresolved or disputed cases.

    Settlement. The process by which contractual obligations are discharged and collateral or payouts become available. A market can be economically decided before settlement is complete.

    Sharpness. Concentration of predictive distributions. Sharpness is useful in conjunction with calibration; confident wrong forecasts are not improved forecasts.

    Slippage. The difference between a stated execution reference and the eventual fill price under a defined convention. Do not count the bid/ask spread twice by subtracting it again after using executable prices.

    Survivorship bias. Analysis based only on entities or records that remain observable under a selection mechanism. Markets disappearing from a live endpoint may require an historical endpoint rather than an inference that they never existed.

    Synthetic fixture. Intentionally generated data used to illustrate a mechanism or exercise code. It must be labeled, reproducible, and kept distinct from evidence of historical performance.

    Untouched evaluation. Data withheld from all decisions that define a reported model or strategy until a predeclared final assessment. Repeatedly inspecting it and adjusting the process destroys the intended separation.

    Vintage. A version of a data release as it existed at a particular publication time. Using a latest revised series in place of the contemporary vintage can introduce information unavailable to historical decision-makers.

    Walk-forward evaluation. A sequence of model-fitting and evaluation steps ordered through time, with explicit information cutoffs. It does not automatically solve dependence, tuning, or repeated-testing problems.

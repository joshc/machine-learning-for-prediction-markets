    Machine Learning for Prediction Markets¶

    Probabilities, Evidence, and Execution¶

    An original, practical textbook for readers who can write Python and know basic probability.

    Prediction-market trading is not just classification with a dollar sign attached. The target is a contractual payout; information arrives at particular times; prices reflect incentives and market structure; an order may never fill; and cash can remain committed after an event becomes obvious. A model that predicts outcomes accurately can still lose money. A trader who makes money can still have a bad model and an unrepeatable result.

    This book connects those distinctions rather than treating them as footnotes. It begins with what the contract promises, develops probability and data discipline, introduces machine learning only after baselines and evaluation, and then studies execution, evidence, portfolios, and paper operations. Its last five chapters offer practical workflows, not a promise to earn a dollar.

    How to read this book¶

    Read Chapters 01-10 in order. They supply the definitions on which the rest depends. Chapters 11-16 develop modeling choices; their purpose is to help you choose the simplest defensible model, not to make every problem a neural-network problem. Chapters 17-24 ask whether forecasts can survive actual trading constraints. Chapter 25 proposes research directions with explicit falsification criteria. Chapters 26-30 turn the preceding ideas into reproducible exercises and a conditional manual decision checklist.

    The reader-facing support files are notation, glossary, references, and exercise navigation. The companion README contains installation instructions. The research ledger explains what was inspected and which claims remain limited by their evidence.

    Book and companion working directories¶

    This self-contained book directory is documentation and educational material within the trading-system repository. The repository README states the trading-system project's purpose; the book's paper-only operating boundary applies to its educational companion, not to every future system component at the Git root.

    Run chapter examples, companion tests, the offline NOAA study, and optional public-snapshot commands from the companion project root. Starting at the trading repository root:

    Set-Location book\companion

    Create and use .venv in that directory as described in the companion setup. Relative paths such as examples\payoffs.py, experiments\noaa_study.py, and data\fixtures\recurring_events.csv in runnable chapter instructions are relative to book\companion. Change directory once, not before every subsequent example in the same shell.

    Manuscript maintenance is separate and uses standard-library Python from the trading repository root, not from book\companion:

    python book\scripts\assemble_claims.py --check
python book\scripts\manuscript_inventory.py
python -m unittest discover -s book\tests -q

    The book's evidence contract¶

    Four kinds of statements appear in this book, and they do different jobs.

    A derivation establishes what follows from specified assumptions. It can be completely correct while its assumptions are a poor description of a real market. A no-arbitrage result for compatible contracts does not establish compatibility for two headlines that happen to sound similar.

    An empirical finding describes a particular population, sample, measurement procedure, and historical period. Its relevance to another venue must be argued, not presumed. Results about forecasting accuracy are not automatically results about executable profit.

    A synthetic illustration deliberately invents data to isolate a mechanism. It can demonstrate accounting, a model failure, or a statistical trap. It cannot demonstrate that a real exchange offers an economic opportunity. The companion's default offline examples are labeled teaching fixtures for this reason.

    A hypothesis is a question worth investigating. It earns its place through a plausible mechanism, a relationship to existing work, and a test that could reject it. It is not made true by a sophisticated architecture or a persuasive narrative.

    Citation links identify the sources that support claims. They are not decoration or a guarantee that every assumption has been satisfied. The source ledger distinguishes publication status, access depth, and limitations. Official documentation describes a product at the time it was inspected, not a permanent contract with future API users.

    The practical boundary¶

    The executable learning path is paper-only. No program in the book should submit a funded order, sign a wallet transaction, transfer collateral, or silently fall back to production trading. A paper result can be useful even when the final decision is to abstain.

    The final chapter addresses readers who may independently consider a small manual live trade. They must be eligible, understand the exact rules, recheck current fees and execution conditions, and be able to afford the loss. The book supplies an analytical checklist, not personal financial or legal advice.

    The phrase "first dollar" is a motivating goal, not a statistical guarantee. A strategy can have positive expected value and lose its next trade; a negative-value strategy can win. What matters is whether a predeclared process produces credible evidence under honest assumptions and remains within a risk budget.

    Why the order matters¶

    flowchart LR
    A[Contract and rules] --> B[Information available at decision]
    B --> C[Probability forecast]
    C --> D[Evaluation and uncertainty]
    D --> E[Executable costs and constraints]
    E --> F[Paper decisions and accounting]
    F --> G[Evidence review or abstention]

    There is deliberately no automatic arrow from a favorable backtest to a live order. The missing arrow is a human decision under uncertainty, not a feature that the software forgot to implement.

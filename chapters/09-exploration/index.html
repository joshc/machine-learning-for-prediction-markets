    9. Exploration without fooling yourself¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Read Chapter 7 and Chapter 8 first. You should already be comfortable with dependence groups, the seven clocks, and the available and grouped_chronological_split mechanics. This chapter assumes basic descriptive statistics: means, variances, and simple correlation, at the level of an introductory course.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to: state what exploratory analysis is for and what it must never be used to justify; apply a feature-availability gate before any exploratory plot or summary; recognize when a naive descriptive statistic silently treats dependent observations as independent, and correct for it at the level of the summary itself; distinguish a predeclared inclusion rule from a convenience filter chosen after looking at results; build a catalog of leakage patterns specific to the exploratory phase; and design a frozen confirmatory analysis that is genuinely protected from everything that came before it.

    9.1 What exploration is allowed to do¶

    Exploratory data analysis exists to help a researcher understand structure: what a variable's distribution looks like, whether an expected relationship shows up at all, where data quality problems live, and which modeling choices are even plausible given what the data can support. It generates hypotheses and informs design decisions. It is an essential, legitimate stage of research.

    What exploration must never do is quietly become the evidence for the claim a later chapter reports as a result. If a researcher tries several candidate features, several thresholds, and several groupings while looking at how each choice affects apparent performance on the same records that will later be called a "test set," the resulting favorable configuration is not evidence that the configuration works; it is evidence that a sufficiently flexible search process, given enough attempts, can find something that looks good on a fixed sample by chance. This is the essence of the glossary's definition of multiple testing, and it applies just as much to informal visual inspection and ad hoc feature engineering as it does to a documented grid search over model hyperparameters.

    The discipline this chapter asks for is not "do not explore." It is: decide, in advance, which portion of your data exploration is allowed to touch, and treat any subsequent confirmatory claim as valid only to the extent that the untouched portion was genuinely never inspected, adjusted for, or used to steer a choice before that claim was finalized.

    9.2 The first gate: is this feature even legitimately visible?¶

    Before a predictive feature is plotted as something a historical decision could use, check that it was observable by that row's decision cutoff. Labels obey a different clock: they need to be available by the research or model-fitting cutoff at which they are consumed, not by their own earlier forecast times. Retrospective exploration of training outcomes is legitimate; using eventual test outcomes to design the model is not. Keeping these roles separate prevents both leakage and the mistaken requirement that a forecaster already know its target.

    Consider a concrete illustrative mistake. A researcher, while poking at a dataset built from the recurring-event fixture, computes a new candidate summary feature: "the variance of the invented signal across both of this event's recorded snapshots." This sounds like an innocuous descriptive statistic. But an event's later, three-hour-horizon snapshot is only available close to the event itself; a decision made at the twelve-hour horizon cannot possibly know the value that will be recorded three hours before the event, because that later value does not exist yet at the earlier decision time. A "variance across both snapshots" feature computed and then attached to the twelve-hour row has silently pulled information from after that row's own decision cutoff. The fix is not clever statistics; it is running exactly the same features_available check on every derived, engineered exploratory feature that the pipeline already runs on raw fields, before that feature is allowed into any plot, correlation table, or model input, confirmatory or otherwise.

    The same discipline applies to summaries that never become model inputs. Looking at the future test period can steer later choices even without an explicit numeric feature entering a fit. On the allowed exploratory period, however, an audit may deliberately compare first and later vintages or examine eventual outcomes to diagnose data quality. Label that work retrospective and keep those later values out of earlier predictive features.

    9.3 Repeated observations are not repeated independent evidence¶

    The recurring-event fixture stores 180 invented events, each with two snapshot rows, for 360 rows. To isolate how a sample-count error affects uncertainty, consider the familiar standard-error approximation \(1/\sqrt{n-3}\) for Fisher's transformed correlation, \(z=\tfrac12\log((1+r)/(1-r))\), under independent bivariate-normal sampling. This is not the standard error of the raw correlation \(r\), and the fixture's binary labels do not satisfy that bivariate-normal model. Used only as an arithmetic illustration, plugging in \(n=360\) gives

    \[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{360-3}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{357}}\approx 0.053. \]

    Duplicating each of 180 genuinely independent pairs does not create 360 independent pairs. Under the illustration's sampling assumptions, the standard error would still be

    \[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{180-3}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{177}}\approx 0.075. \]

    The ratio is about \(0.075/0.053\approx1.42\): the appropriate standard error in this illustration is about 42 percent larger than the duplicated-row calculation. This is not a measured interval for the fixture's signal-label correlation. Dependence does not universally increase variance: the direction depends on covariances of the statistic's contributions, and negative dependence can reduce it. Nor are 180 and 360 universal lower and upper effective-sample-size bounds. For the companion, one predeclared latest snapshot per event makes the weighting and unit explicit; for an actual correlation claim with binary outcomes, use a justified event-level or grouped uncertainty calculation rather than importing this illustrative normal-theory formula.

    The shipped examples\forecast_models.py script shows this same dependency concretely, with an actually observed pair of numbers rather than a hypothetical one. It rescoring a single already-fitted logistic model against both of one specific event's decision-time snapshots, event toy-0135, produces 0.258916 at the twelve-hour horizon and 0.258227 at the three-hour horizon. Those two numbers come from one frozen model evaluated twice, not from two independently trained forecasters, and they describe one realized event whose outcome will eventually be a single shared label; an exploratory summary that averaged or compared these two rows as though they were separate pieces of evidence about two different events would repeat exactly the Section 9.3 mistake with real, not invented, output. It is also worth being precise about what this rescoring is not: it is a fixed model's output evaluated at two different input snapshots, not a fitted sequence model that learns how a forecast should evolve as a horizon shortens, and the companion's own reported limitation for this demonstration says as much, that its early-horizon calibration is unvalidated.

    9.4 Selection during exploration¶

    A closely related trap is choosing, mid-exploration, to restrict attention to "the interesting cases": markets with enough volume to be liquid, events that actually resolved by the time you looked, or records with no missing fields. Each of these restrictions can be entirely reasonable. The problem is deciding on them only after seeing how they affect a result you already have an opinion about.

    The remedy from the previous two chapters carries over directly: write down the inclusion rule, and its justification, before computing the statistic it will be used to filter. If a liquidity threshold is later found to change the qualitative conclusion, report both the filtered and unfiltered pictures rather than quietly keeping only the one that supports the conclusion you started with. Keep a record of every filtering rule considered during exploration, not just the one eventually adopted; this record becomes essential input to the multiple-testing discussion below, because an unreported sequence of "what if we exclude these" attempts is exactly the kind of informal search that invalidates a naive significance claim on whatever filter is finally chosen.

    9.5 A leakage catalog specific to exploration¶

    Three exploration-specific leakage patterns deserve their own names, because each one can occur even when a researcher is being careful about the basic availability gate from Section 9.2.

    Aggregated future-looking features. A summary statistic computed "over the whole event" rather than "as of the decision cutoff" quietly imports information from after that cutoff, exactly as in the Section 9.2 example. This pattern is common because aggregation feels like a modeling convenience rather than an information-flow decision.

    A temporal gap is not a leakage cure. It is tempting to believe that if a training row's decision time is months before a test row's decision time, the two rows cannot possibly share information. That belief is false whenever the two rows belong to the same dependence group, or to two different groups that share an underlying settlement source, as in the cross-venue certification example from Chapter 7. A nine-hour gap between an event's twelve-hour and three-hour snapshots does not make them independent; they share one outcome and one label-availability time regardless of how far apart their decision timestamps sit. grouped_chronological_split's behavior reflects exactly this: it purges an entire dependence group that straddles a cutoff rather than trusting decision-time separation alone to protect the split.

    Repeated informal inspection of the confirmatory cohort. Even without adjusting any code, looking at descriptive plots of what will later be called the test set, and letting that look influence any subsequent choice about features, thresholds, or model family, quietly consumes some of that cohort's evidentiary value. The formal literature on this problem is directly relevant here. White's reality check for data snooping addresses inference after a search over many candidate rules; the false discovery rate framework of Benjamini and Hochberg addresses the risk of reporting an impressive result out of many tested hypotheses; and Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado, and Zhu's probability-of-backtest-overfitting work is specifically concerned with the danger of repeatedly consulting a supposedly held-out sample during model or strategy development.     9.6 Designing a frozen confirmatory analysis¶

    The practical fix for all of the above is to split the research process itself into two stages with a hard boundary between them, not merely to split the data.

    In the exploratory stage, work only with the training and validation cohorts established by a predeclared, availability-respecting split. Use this stage to understand distributions, check for the leakage patterns above, settle on a small number of candidate feature definitions and model families, and write down, before looking at the confirmatory cohort at all, the exact metric, the exact cohort definition, and the exact comparison that will be reported as the confirmatory result.

    In the confirmatory stage, apply that exact, predeclared procedure to the test cohort exactly once. If the result invites a follow-up question, that follow-up is a new piece of exploratory work requiring its own, separately protected confirmatory data, not a second look at the same test cohort with a slightly adjusted procedure. This is a demanding standard, and it is routinely violated in casual research precisely because a single additional look feels harmless in isolation. Its cost is cumulative: each additional look at the same nominally held-out data increases the chance that whatever appears favorable is an artifact of that look count rather than a genuine property of the underlying process, which is the shared concern behind the reality-check, false-discovery-rate, and backtest-overfitting literatures cited above.

    9.7 A worked exploratory-to-confirmatory walkthrough¶

    Put the pieces together with one concrete, fully disclosed walkthrough using the synthetic fixture's own cutoffs: training through April 1, validation through May 16, and test through July 1, 2025. During exploration, a researcher restricts every computation to the training and validation cohorts only. Following Section 9.3's lesson, the researcher first collapses each event down to its single latest available snapshot using latest_per_event, producing one row per event rather than two. Following Section 9.2's lesson, the researcher checks that every candidate feature passes features_available before it is even plotted. Following Section 9.4's lesson, the researcher writes down, before looking at any performance number, that the planned comparison is a fixed logistic model against the fixed overall training-cohort event rate, scored by the single-YES Brier score defined formally in the next chapter, on the test cohort's own predeclared latest-snapshot rows.

    Only after the plan is fixed does the hypothetical researcher compute the final comparison. An unfavorable result is reported, not used to select a replacement feature set on the same test cohort. The companion enforces part of that discipline: evaluate_forecaster rejects overlap with both fitted event IDs and fitted dependence-group IDs, and selects one latest row per event. It cannot determine whether a human already inspected the test outcomes or chose the design after seeing them. The reproducible toy run illustrates the mechanics, not a software certificate of untouched research.

    9.8 Assumptions and failure modes¶

    Assumption: an engineered summary feature respects the same cutoff as its raw inputs. Failure mode: aggregating "across the whole event" silently reaches past the decision cutoff that governs one particular row, as in the cross-snapshot variance example.

    Assumption: a wide time gap between two rows is sufficient to treat them as independent. Failure mode: shared dependence groups, shared settlement sources, or shared underlying processes can make two temporally distant rows highly dependent; the gap alone proves nothing.

    Assumption: informal visual inspection of a test cohort is harmless because no code was changed. Failure mode: a researcher's subsequent choices, made with that inspection in mind even unconsciously, are no longer independent of the outcome being evaluated, undermining the confirmatory claim exactly as a documented parameter search would.

    Assumption: a convenience filter chosen after seeing preliminary results is equivalent to a predeclared inclusion rule. Failure mode: the filter becomes an unreported extra degree of freedom in the analysis, inflating the apparent strength of whatever conclusion survives it.

    Assumption: one favorable confirmatory result licenses an additional look at the same test cohort to refine it. Failure mode: each additional look degrades the very property, being untouched before finalization, that made the original result meaningful in the first place.

    Exercises and solution sketches¶

    Exercise 1: catching a future-looking feature¶

    A researcher proposes a feature: "the number of snapshots eventually recorded for this event." Explain why this fails the Section 9.2 availability gate for an early-horizon row, and propose an available replacement.

    Solution sketch. At the twelve-hour decision point, the process cannot yet know whether a later three-hour snapshot will exist; that count is only knowable once the event's full recording history is complete, which is after the decision. An available replacement is "the number of snapshots recorded for this event strictly before the current decision time," which is zero or a count using only what already exists at decision time.

    Exercise 2: correcting an inflated correlation claim¶

    A colleague reports a highly significant correlation between a feature and an outcome, computed over 500 rows, and states the standard error using \(n=500\). You learn the 500 rows come from only 210 distinct events, each contributing on average roughly 2.4 rows. What should you ask for before accepting the significance claim, and what is the qualitative direction of the correction?

    Solution sketch. Ask for the observation weighting, a predeclared event-level summary, and an uncertainty calculation justified for the actual dependence structure. Repeated positively correlated contributions commonly make the naive standard error too small, but the direction is not guaranteed for every statistic or covariance pattern. Collapsing rows can also change the estimand and point estimate, so it is not merely replacing 500 with 210 in an otherwise unchanged formula.

    Exercise 3: an embargo is not a cure¶

    A pipeline adds a thirty-day embargo between its training cutoff and its test cutoff, discarding any row whose decision time falls in that window. Does this guarantee that no training row and test row share an outcome-relevant dependence? Justify your answer using the definitions in this chapter.

    Solution sketch. No. An embargo removes rows adjacent in decision time, but two rows can share a dependence group, or share an underlying settlement source, without their decision times being adjacent at all, as in the cross-venue certification example. The embargo helps with certain short-range contamination patterns but does nothing by itself to prevent a shared-event or shared-label leak that spans a much larger time gap.

    Exercise 4: one look too many¶

    After a predeclared confirmatory test shows the candidate model underperforming the base rate, a researcher proposes trying two alternative feature sets against the same test cohort "just to see." What should happen instead, and why?

    Solution sketch. The predeclared result should be reported as the finding, including the underperformance, since a negative or inconclusive result is a legitimate outcome. Any further feature sets should be evaluated only against a newly designated, previously untouched confirmatory cohort, or clearly labeled as renewed exploratory work whose conclusions require their own future confirmatory test; reusing the same test cohort a second time turns a confirmatory claim into an informally searched one.

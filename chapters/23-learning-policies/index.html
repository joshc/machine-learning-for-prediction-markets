    23. Learning execution policies in simulation¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Read the forecast-to-order decision, a backtest that respects execution, evidence after strategy search, and Chapters 21-22 before this chapter. You should be comfortable with conditional expectation, the notion of a decision cutoff and label availability from the notation registry, and the glossary entries for leakage, selection bias, and untouched evaluation.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: formalize state, action, and reward for a simple one-step wait-or-buy decision; derive the tabular greedy learning rule and explain why it needs full context-action support; derive the inverse-propensity-scoring (IPS) estimator from an importance-sampling identity and explain what it requires to be valid; compute and interpret an effective sample size as a variance diagnostic; explain why evaluating a learned policy under a shifted regime is a genuinely different test from evaluating it on more data from the same regime; state precisely why reward timing rules out any lookahead; and explain, with a concrete numeric contrast, why a simulator's reward estimate is not evidence of real trading profit.

    23.1 State, action, and reward for a wait-or-buy decision¶

    A contextual bandit observes a context, chooses an action, and receives a reward. Here \(S\in\{0,1\}\) is an invented feature bucket and \(A\in\{\text{wait},\text{buy}\}\). To match conventional bandit notation, this chapter uses \(R\) locally for net reward, not the notation registry's terminal contract payout; the settlement outcome is \(Y\), and buying earns \(R=Y-0.51\). Likewise \(b(a\mid s)\) below denotes the behavior-policy probability, not an executable bid.

    Define the reward explicitly. Waiting always yields \(R=0\): no cash is committed, and (for now) no opportunity cost is charged for having declined an available trade — a simplification flagged in Section 23.6. Buying commits to an all-in invented cost of USD 0.51 per unit for this chapter's own simulator (a distinct, explicitly labeled convention, not to be confused with the book's shared canonical USD 0.41 accounting example used in Chapters 21-22); the reward is \(R = Y - 0.51\) if the underlying binary settlement is \(Y\), and \(0\) if the action was wait. This is a one-step episode: one context, one action, one reward, with no sequence of states evolving afterward. That simplicity is deliberate and is itself a limitation, addressed in Section 23.6.

    Given a context-conditional probability \(p_s = P(Y=1\mid S=s)\), the expected reward of each action is immediate:

    \[ \mathbb{E}[R\mid S=s, A=\text{buy}] = p_s - 0.51, \qquad \mathbb{E}[R\mid S=s, A=\text{wait}] = 0. \]

    Suppose \(p_0=0.35\) and \(p_1=0.65\) (two invented, distinct context-conditional success rates). Then \(\mathbb{E}[R\mid S{=}0,\text{buy}] = 0.35-0.51 = -0.16\), so waiting (reward 0) dominates buying in context 0. In context 1, \(\mathbb{E}[R\mid S{=}1,\text{buy}] = 0.65-0.51 = 0.14 > 0\), so buying dominates waiting. The optimal deterministic policy is therefore \(\pi^{*}(0)=\text{wait}\), \(\pi^{*}(1)=\text{buy}\) — a policy this chapter's companion example actually recovers from logged data, as Section 23.8 shows.

    23.2 Learning a tabular policy from a behavior-policy log¶

    A real decision-maker does not know \(p_s\) directly; it must be estimated from a log of past decisions and their observed rewards. The log is generated by a behavior policy: whatever process (a fixed rule, a human, or a randomizer) actually chose the logged actions. A simple and instructive behavior policy logs actions uniformly at random with a known propensity — the probability the behavior policy assigned to the action it happened to take, for example \(b(a\mid s)=0.5\) for either action in either context, exactly the policy used to generate this chapter's simulated log.

    Given such a log, a tabular learning rule estimates \(\mathbb{E}[R\mid S{=}s,A{=}a]\) by the empirical mean reward among logged rows sharing that exact context and action, then picks the action with the higher empirical mean in each context. This rule requires full support: every one of the four (context, action) combinations must actually appear in the log, or there is no data to estimate one of the means from, and the honest response is to refuse to produce a policy for that context rather than silently guessing. A behavior policy that never tries a particular action in a particular context makes that action's value permanently unlearnable from this log, no matter how large the log otherwise is — a first, simple instance of the off-policy support requirement developed further below.

    23.3 Evaluating a policy without deploying it: the importance-sampling identity¶

    Suppose a policy \(\pi\) has been learned (Section 23.2), and the question is how well it would have performed, without actually running it live. If the log was generated by a different policy — the behavior policy \(b\) — a naive average of logged rewards answers the wrong question, because it reflects \(b\)'s action choices, not \(\pi\)'s. Inverse propensity scoring (IPS) corrects for this with an importance-sampling reweighting. Define, for each logged row \(i\) with context \(S_i\), action \(A_i\), reward \(R_i\), and known propensity \(b(A_i\mid S_i)\), the estimator

    \[ \hat V(\pi) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\mathbf 1\{A_i=\pi(S_i)\}}{b(A_i\mid S_i)}\,R_i. \]

    To see why this targets \(\mathbb{E}[R\mid S,\pi(S)]\), condition on a fixed context \(s\) and take the expectation of one term over the behavior policy's own action distribution:

    \[ \mathbb{E}_b\!\left[\frac{\mathbf 1\{A=\pi(s)\}}{b(A\mid s)}R \,\middle|\, S=s\right] = \sum_{a} b(a\mid s)\cdot\frac{\mathbf 1\{a=\pi(s)\}}{b(a\mid s)}\cdot \mathbb{E}[R\mid S=s, A=a] = \mathbb{E}[R\mid S=s, A=\pi(s)], \]

    because only the target-action term survives. Averaging over contexts targets the policy's reward in the logging population. For the causal "what if we used this action" interpretation, the logged reward distribution must also match the reward that action would produce under the target policy: no hidden action-selection confounding after conditioning on the recorded context, and no unmodeled interference or policy-induced change in the reward mechanism. Randomized actions in this simulator satisfy that requirement by construction. A human log with a recorded propensity field does not establish it automatically.

    Unbiasedness here treats \(\pi\) as fixed independently of the evaluation outcomes and requires positive behavior probability for every target action. Evaluating a policy on the same rewards used to select it reintroduces selection bias. Doubly robust methods combine reward and behavior-policy models under their own assumptions; they are related work, not implemented here and not a remedy for missing support. DUDIK2011

    23.4 Effective sample size: a variance diagnostic, not a bias fix¶

    The IPS estimator's variance depends on how uneven the importance weights \(1/b(A_i\mid S_i)\) are; if many logged rows receive weight zero (because their action differs from what the target policy would choose) and the rest carry a large uniform weight, the effective number of independent observations behind the estimate is smaller than the raw row count \(n\). A standard diagnostic is the effective sample size,

    \[ \mathrm{ESS} = \frac{\left(\sum_i w_i\right)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}, \qquad w_i = \frac{\mathbf 1\{A_i=\pi(S_i)\}}{b(A_i\mid S_i)}. \]

    If nonzero weights are equal, this ESS equals their count; unequal weights reduce it. With uniform two-action logging, 409 matching rows out of 800 give ESS 409. This is a weight-concentration diagnostic, not an exact effective sample size for every reward distribution and not a correction for event dependence. Reward variance, dependence, and the relationship between reward and weight also affect the estimator's uncertainty. A healthy ESS does not prove unbiasedness, adequate support in every region, or simulator realism.

    23.5 Same regime versus a shifted regime: two different questions¶

    Holding out a fresh batch of logged rounds generated by the same simulator settings tests whether the learned policy generalizes within the regime it was trained on. Holding out a batch generated under a deliberately shifted regime — for instance, subtracting a fixed amount from every context's success probability, representing a stylized "conditions got worse" scenario — tests something categorically different: whether a policy learned under one regime remains sensible under another. Because \(\pi^{*}\) was derived in Section 23.1 from \(p_0=0.35, p_1=0.65\), and the shifted regime moves both down by 0.30 to \(0.05\) and \(0.35\), the previously profitable context-1 buy action now has expected reward \(0.35-0.51=-0.16\), no longer positive; the fixed action mapping learned under the old regime is not the optimal mapping under the new one, yet the policy still executes the old mapping because nothing in this one-step setup detects the shift. A same-simulator held-out evaluation and a shifted-regime held-out evaluation must be reported separately and neither substitutes for the other: a policy that looks robust only because it was re-tested on more of the same conditions has not been tested against a train/test market regime change at all, and a change in market regime (a shift in liquidity, information flow, or participant composition) is exactly the kind of real-world discontinuity a fixed historical evaluation window cannot promise to represent.

    Reusing one learned policy, one simulator, and one train/test split repeatedly while quietly trying several regime definitions until a favorable-looking comparison appears is a form of the selection problem examined in backtest-overfitting research; recording every regime and split actually tried, not only the reported one, is necessary before treating any single comparison as informative. BAILEY2017

    23.6 Reward-arrival timing: no lookahead, ever¶

    The one-step simulator generates the settlement and reward within the same loop iteration. Real rewards may become known long after an action. A reward may enter fitting only when its availability time is no later than that fitting cutoff; it need not be known at its own earlier action time. Later evaluation of a frozen action against its eventual reward is legitimate. Using that reward to alter the earlier action, its features, or the policy that supposedly chose it is not.

    A policy retrained during a log must therefore wait for each training reward's actual availability. Repeatedly improving a policy in the same simulator is a separate risk: it can optimize simulator quirks. Use a held-out evaluation log and prespecified stress regimes rather than reporting only rewards that helped choose the policy.

    23.7 Missing support must be rejected, not extrapolated¶

    Distinguish two failures. If the behavior policy gives a target action zero probability in a relevant context, population support fails and the IPS identity cannot identify that action's value. If the probability is positive but the finite sample happens to contain no matching action, the observed weights are zero, not undefined; ordinary IPS remains mathematically defined, but that sample contains no direct reward evidence for the missing pair.

    The companion adopts a stricter finite-sample gate: ips_value refuses to report a value unless every target context-action pair appears at least once. This is an explicit teaching safeguard, not a theorem that IPS weights become undefined whenever a random sample misses a pair. One occurrence also does not ensure adequate precision. Fixed-batch RL literature motivates avoiding unsupported extrapolation but supplies no market-execution guarantee. FUJIMOTO2019

    23.8 Why a simulator's IPS reward is not real profit¶

    Even a fully correct IPS estimate, computed on a log with adequate support and a healthy effective sample size, only answers "what would this target policy have earned under exactly this simulator's assumptions." Several of those assumptions are chosen for teaching clarity and do not describe a real market. Rewards here are independent across rounds, with no shared-event dependence, no fees beyond the flat invented USD 0.51 cost, no order depth, no partial fills, no latency, and no queue competition. Contexts are two artificial buckets rather than a genuine, high-dimensional feature vector. Propensities are known exactly by construction, because the behavior policy was built to log them; a real historical decision log rarely comes with exact, verified logging probabilities attached, and estimating them introduces its own error that plain IPS does not account for. None of the diagnostics in this chapter — support checks, effective sample size, or a shifted-regime comparison — can certify that the simulator itself resembles any actual venue's mechanics; they only certify that the estimator is behaving correctly given the simulator it was handed. A favorable IPS number is evidence about a specific labeled simulation, not about a tradable real-world edge.

    23.9 Assumptions and failure modes¶

    This chapter's derivations assume a single decision round with an immediately resolved reward, a known and strictly positive logging propensity for every action a target policy would ever choose, independent rounds with no shared-outcome dependence, and a policy space restricted to deterministic, per-context action choices. Violating the positive-propensity assumption breaks the importance-sampling identity outright, not merely its accuracy. Violating round independence (for instance, if several logged rounds actually referenced the same underlying real-world event) would make the effective sample size calculation overstate genuine informational content, similar to the event-grouping cautions from earlier chapters. Extending this framework to a genuine multi-step reinforcement-learning setting, with fills, latency, and queueing folded into a state that evolves over time, requires substantially more machinery than the tabular one-step case shown here and is not attempted in this chapter.

    23.10 Companion example¶

    The companion script policy_learning.py builds an 800-round logged bandit under the Section 23.1 context-success probabilities, learns a tabular policy, and evaluates it two ways: on a fresh same-simulator held-out log, and on a log generated under the Section 23.5 adverse-selection-shifted regime. The learned policy is wait in context 0 and buy in context 1, exactly matching Section 23.1's derivation. The same-simulator held-out evaluation reports an IPS reward per round of approximately 0.091175 with an effective sample size of about 409 out of 800 logged rounds; the shifted-regime evaluation reports approximately minus 0.088825 at the same effective sample size, concretely illustrating Section 23.5's point that the same fixed policy performs oppositely once the underlying regime changes. A separate call demonstrates the missing-support rejection from Section 23.7 using a two-row log that never tries the buy action in either context. Run it with:

    Command working directory: run this chapter's Python commands from book\companion. From the trading repository root, run Set-Location book\companion once first. The .venv belongs to that companion project; see companion setup.

    .\.venv\Scripts\python.exe examples\policy_learning.py

    The companion's own output explicitly labels its rewards synthetic and notes that IPS needs known propensities and support, not merely logged prices; nothing in this example is presented as measured trading performance.

    Exercises and solution sketches¶

    Exercise 1: a third context¶

    Add a third context with \(p_2 = 0.20\) to Section 23.1's setup, using the same USD 0.51 all-in cost. What is the optimal action in this context, and why?

    Solution sketch. \(\mathbb{E}[R\mid S{=}2,\text{buy}] = 0.20-0.51=-0.31 < 0 = \mathbb{E}[R\mid S{=}2,\text{wait}]\), so waiting is optimal, by an even larger margin than in context 0.

    Exercise 2: unbiasedness with three actions¶

    Sketch, without full algebra, why the importance-sampling identity in Section 23.3 still holds if there are three possible actions per context instead of two, provided the target policy still chooses one deterministic action per context.

    Solution sketch. The sum in the derivation ranges over however many actions the behavior policy can choose; the indicator still zeroes out every term except \(a=\pi(s)\), and the \(b(a\mid s)\) factors still cancel for that surviving term. The number of actions does not affect the cancellation, only how many terms exist before it.

    Exercise 3: computing an effective sample size¶

    A held-out log of 4 rows has importance weights \((2, 2, 0, 0)\) for a given policy. Compute the effective sample size and compare it to the raw row count.

    Solution sketch. \(\mathrm{ESS} = (2+2+0+0)^2/(2^2+2^2+0+0) = 16/8 = 2\). The effective sample size equals the count of nonzero, equal-weight rows (2), well below the raw count of 4, because the two zero-weight rows contribute nothing.

    Exercise 4: why missing support cannot be patched with an average¶

    A held-out log never tries the buy action in context 1, but the target policy chooses buy there. Explain why substituting the overall average reward from other contexts is not an acceptable fix.

    Solution sketch. The importance-sampling identity relies on reweighting actual observations of the action in question in that specific context; an average reward from unrelated context-action pairs answers a different conditional expectation entirely and provides no evidence, unbiased or otherwise, about \(\mathbb{E}[R\mid S{=}1,\text{buy}]\). The honest response is to report that this context-action pair is unsupported, not to substitute an unrelated number.

    Exercise 5: why a positive shifted-regime IPS number still would not license live trading¶

    Suppose a modified policy is learned that shows a positive IPS reward on the shifted-regime held-out log as well as the original one. Give two specific reasons this result alone would not justify moving to real trading.

    Solution sketch. The reward function still assumes one flat invented cost with no depth, latency, fees, or queueing, so it does not represent an executable price path; and the shifted regime is a single, hand-chosen perturbation, not a demonstrated model of real regime change, so success against it does not establish robustness to whatever an actual future regime shift would look like.

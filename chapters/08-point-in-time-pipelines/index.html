    8. Building a point-in-time pipeline¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Read Chapter 7 first: this chapter assumes you already accept that an event family, an instance, a contract, and a rule version must be tracked separately, and that a dataset's provenance category must be stated honestly. You should be comfortable with UTC timestamps and simple set/interval reasoning. No new probability theory is required yet; this chapter is about what a decision is allowed to see, not about how to model it.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to: name and distinguish seven clocks relevant to one forecasting decision; explain, with a worked timeline, why "download the latest data" silently violates a point-in-time constraint; describe why a paginated, cutoff-partitioned API requires deliberate query design rather than a single bulk request; explain what a snapshot-based order-book feed can and cannot establish about history; and specify the minimum provenance record a disciplined collector must keep. You should also be able to trace, in the companion's actual validation code, how an availability check and a grouped chronological split enforce these constraints mechanically rather than by convention alone.

    8.1 Seven clocks for one decision¶

    A single forecasting decision touches more moments in time than casual description usually admits. This book keeps seven of them separate, because collapsing any two of them into one silently changes what a study claims to have shown.

    The event time is when the real-world matter the contract is about actually occurs or is realized, for example the moment a measurement is taken or a vote is certified. The source publication time is when some outside publisher first releases information relevant to the event, which can be before the event (an announced schedule), at the event, or well after it (a delayed release of a measurement). The local observation time is when your own system actually receives and records that publication; a fast publisher and a slow ingestion pipeline can create meaningful separation here that a naive schema ignores. The decision time is the instant your process commits to a forecast or an action, and it is the single most important cutoff: everything used to produce that decision must have been available, in the sense defined below, no later than this moment. The fill time is when an actual order is matched, which can be simultaneous with the decision (aggressive marketable order) or arbitrarily later, or never (a resting order that never executes). The label availability time is when the outcome needed to score or train on this decision becomes knowable, which need not coincide with either the event time or any settlement action. The settlement time is when contractual funds or payouts are actually and finally transferred, which can trail label availability by a dispute or processing window.

    None of these seven moments needs to equal any other, and a pipeline that assumes two of them coincide by default is choosing a shortcut, not a law of nature.

    A concrete worked timeline¶

    The companion's synthetic recurring-event fixture makes a specific, disclosed choice among these clocks, and walking through its actual numbers is a useful way to see the distinctions operate rather than merely list them. For one invented event, the event time is set at 18:00 UTC on a given day. The fixture's decision time is set either twelve or three hours before that event time, representing two snapshots of the same underlying event. For the twelve-hour snapshot, the invented bulletin's publication time is ten minutes before that decision time, and the local observation time is two minutes before the decision. The invented outcome's label availability time is set twelve hours after the event time. All of this is drawn from the same generator used by examples\point_in_time.py, and every one of these fields is an explicit column in the stored fixture, not an inferred convention.

    Two lessons follow immediately from writing the numbers down this way. First, publication (ten minutes before decision) and observation (two minutes before decision) are both comfortably before the decision cutoff in this invented design, so both clocks pass an availability check; the fixture also includes, in its own example script, a deliberately constructed "late" row whose observation time is moved to one second after the decision time, and that row is correctly rejected by the availability check. Second, the twelve-hour and three-hour rows for the same event share one outcome and one label availability time, twelve hours after the shared event, even though their decision times differ by nine hours; they must be scored, split, and reported as two dependent glimpses of one event, never as two independent trials.

    8.2 Why "download the latest" breaks the clock¶

    A tempting shortcut is to query a provider's current endpoint and treat its value as though it had always been available. NOAA's documentation warns that later quality-control checks can revise monthly values. NOAA_DATA No particular revision magnitude was measured here. For an invented example, suppose a month's mean was first released as 421.30 parts per million and later revised to 421.34. Using 421.34 as an input to a decision that could only have seen 421.30 leaks future information. Even a small revision can also flip a threshold label, but that raises a separate target-definition question: a contract tied to the initial release must be scored against the initial release, whereas a predeclared final-vintage target can legitimately be scored later against its final outcome. Evaluation labels are normally unknown at prediction time; leakage occurs when those future labels or revisions influence an earlier feature, fit, calibration, or selection decision, not merely because the eventual target was uncertain.

    The general principle is that a data field has a vintage: a version of a record as it existed at a specific publication time. A complete point-in-time archive would store, for every reported value, every vintage and the timestamp at which each vintage became public, so that a historical query can ask "what did this field say as of this specific past date" rather than "what does this field say now." This book's data-feasibility review is explicit that no such vintage archive was established for the NOAA series used in its real-data study; the chosen extract is therefore explicitly a current-vintage retrospective series, and a date-ordered split of it cannot recover the historical vintages a contemporary decision-maker would actually have seen. The honest response is not to pretend the limitation away, but to state it plainly in every place the dataset is used, exactly as the data-feasibility document does, and to avoid any claim that a chronological ordering of a current-vintage series reconstructs historical information availability.

    8.3 Paginated, cutoff-partitioned APIs¶

    A live trading venue's historical interface is rarely a single flat file. It is typically a paginated API: each request returns a bounded page of records plus a cursor or continuation marker, and a complete retrieval for a given window requires repeatedly requesting subsequent pages until the provider signals there are no more. A pipeline that requests only the first page, or that stops as soon as it has "enough" rows for a quick look, has silently applied an undocumented, request-order-dependent selection rule to its own dataset.

    Cutoffs compound this. The inspected Kalshi historical-data documentation describes live and historical partitions governed by moving cutoffs, with different object types, such as market settlement records and trade creation records, governed by different cutoff fields. KALSHI_HISTORY A pipeline that queries only the "live" surface for a window that has since aged past the live cutoff will silently miss records that moved to the historical surface, and a pipeline that assumes every object type shares one cutoff will misdate some of what it retrieves. The general design requirement, independent of any single provider's exact field names, is threefold: identify which surface (live or historical) is authoritative for the requested window before querying; paginate exhaustively rather than opportunistically; and record, for every retrieved record, which surface and which page it came from, so a later audit can distinguish "this record was never observed" from "this record was skipped by an incomplete pagination loop."

    8.4 Order-book gaps and provenance¶

    An order book is not a static object; it is a live, continuously updated structure, and any archived view of it is a snapshot: a picture of the book's state at one moment. The inspected Kalshi order-book documentation describes string-valued fixed-point dollar prices and quantities in YES/NO bid arrays, with ordinary binary contracts implying an ask from the opposite side's bid. KALSHI_BOOK The inspected Polymarket documentation for its public CLOB similarly returns price and size levels alongside a timestamp, a state hash, a minimum order size, and a tick size. POLYMARKET_BOOK Both are genuinely useful interfaces for observing current depth. Neither establishes a complete historical record of every book state that ever existed, and a state hash identifying one returned snapshot is evidence about that snapshot, not proof that no state was missed between two consecutive polls.

    Call the interval between two consecutive recorded snapshots a gap. During a gap, orders can be placed, modified, cancelled, or filled without leaving any trace in a snapshot-based archive; a resting order that appears in one snapshot and is simply absent from the next may have been cancelled, may have been fully filled, or may have been partially filled and then cancelled, and a snapshot-only record generally cannot distinguish these possibilities without an additional trade or fill feed. A disciplined collector therefore has to record, alongside every snapshot, the elapsed time since the previous successfully recorded snapshot for that same book, flag any interval exceeding an expected polling cadence as a gap, and refuse to silently treat a large gap as if it were a normal short interval. This matters directly for anything built on top of the archive: a simulated fill inferred from "the quote was touched at some point between two snapshots" is an assumption about what happened inside a gap, not an observed fact, and the book's glossary is explicit that a resting-order fill must never be inferred from a quote touch alone.

    The minimum provenance record for any collected snapshot, whether from an order book, a trade feed, or a rules page, should include: the exact endpoint and parameters requested; the request time and the response receipt time, both timezone-aware UTC; the raw response content or a verifiable hash of it; the schema or version identifier the provider reports, if any; and, for paginated retrieval, the sequence of cursors used to reach completeness. None of this is exotic engineering. It is the difference between a dataset whose limitations can be audited and one whose limitations can only be guessed at.

    The companion's now-shipped optional prediction_market_lab.market_data module is a concrete, checkable instance of exactly this discipline, not merely an argument for it. Its documented saved-snapshot schema records, for every page, the exact requested URL, the UTC receive time, an explicit historical_point_in_time: false and snapshot_scope: CURRENT_ONLY label, the original response bytes and a hash of them, a separate hash of the parsed payload, and pagination metadata including whether the traversal was truncated and which cursor or offset a continuation would need. It also records what it does not know: the bundle's own publication_history field is explicitly UNKNOWN rather than guessed from the receive time, and eligibility_or_redistribution_rights_verified is recorded as false rather than silently assumed. Every acquisition requires an explicit --acknowledge-terms flag or acknowledge_terms=True construction argument before any network call is made, no default example or test invokes it, and it writes only new, gitignored .snapshot.json files, never an overwrite of an existing one. It is worth being precise about what this module is not: it is not a live broker, it places no orders and accepts no credentials; it is not a historical archive, since snapshot_scope: CURRENT_ONLY is exactly this chapter's warning that a current read is not a historical vintage; and its preserved native fields, Kalshi's string-valued fixed-point dollar/quantity arrays or Polymarket's native bid/ask levels, are kept entirely separate from PaperBroker, which never receives a raw venue payload from this module.

    8.5 How the companion actually enforces these cutoffs¶

    It is worth walking through the actual validation logic. available(published_at, observed_at, cutoff) requires both publication and observation to be at or before the cutoff. Early publication does not help a process that has not received the information, and a receipt timestamp alone cannot make a later publication available sooner. This comparison does not itself validate the ordering or authenticity of the two source timestamps. EventRow.features_available(cutoff) uses the row's decision_at by default. Construction validates field shapes and selected invariants, but does not reject a feature-late row: the deliberately late example can therefore be constructed. The grouped splitter and model feature construction subsequently call the availability check and reject such a row.

    grouped_chronological_split builds on this by additionally purging dependence groups, not individual rows, that straddle a training, validation, or test cutoff. Concretely: for each group identifier, the function computes the earliest and latest decision times among that group's rows. A group is assigned to a cohort only if its entire span of decision times falls within that cohort's boundary and every row's label was available by that cohort's own cutoff; if a group's earliest decision time falls before one cutoff while its latest decision time falls after it, the whole group, not just the straddling rows, is excluded from every cohort and recorded in excluded_groups. Applied to the fixture's teaching split, with training up to April 1, validation up to May 16, and final test up to July 1, 2025, this means the two decision-time snapshots belonging to one invented event are never allowed to land in two different cohorts on the strength of clock position alone; they travel together or they are dropped together. This is the code-level answer to a common mistake: a time gap between two rows, by itself, cannot certify that they are safe to separate, because if they share a label or a dependence group, the gap says nothing about whether that shared information has leaked across the split.

    Running the shipped examples\point_in_time.py against this fixture confirms the mechanism with actual numbers rather than only its logic: of 180 realized events (360 rows), the split assigns 89 events to training, 44 to validation, and 45 to the final test cohort, and it purges exactly two event groups, recorded as toy-0089 and toy-0134, because their decision times or label availability straddled a cohort boundary. Note also that for the fixture's very first row, an explicit check of label_available_at <= decision_at is false, confirming numerically what Section 8.1 argued from the schema alone: this invented label, twelve hours after the event, is never available at either of that event's own decision times, which sit before the event itself. Both facts are observed properties of this one synthetic fixture and its fixed cutoffs; they illustrate the mechanism correctly enforcing itself, not a claim about any real dataset's event counts.

    8.6 The label-availability and settlement clocks are not the same¶

    It is tempting to assume that once an event's outcome is publicly known, the corresponding contract is effectively over. The fixture's own design resists this shortcut by keeping label_available_at as its own field, distinct from any notion of final settlement. In a real venue, the analogous distinction is sharper still: the inspected Polymarket resolution documentation describes a challenge period and a dispute process that can follow an initial proposed outcome, meaning that the economically informative outcome may become knowable well before funds are actually and finally released. POLYMARKET_RESOLUTION A pipeline that conflates "the label is knowable" with "the position has settled" risks understating the capital lockup a strategy would actually experience, since funds committed to a position cannot be redeployed elsewhere until settlement, whatever the state of public knowledge about the outcome.

    The fill clock deserves the same care. A decision to submit an order is not the same event as that order being matched. A marketable order crossing the visible book may fill close to instantaneously; a resting limit order may fill much later, partially, or never. Treating "decision time" and "fill time" as interchangeable silently assumes perfect, immediate execution, which this book's companion explicitly does not assume: its paper-trading fixtures require an explicit user-supplied event to register any fill, precisely so that a fill is never manufactured out of a quote touch or a decision timestamp alone.

    8.7 Assumptions and failure modes¶

    Assumption: publication time equals knowledge time. Failure mode: a publisher can re-release older information, or a report can be embargoed and then released as if new; treating a publication timestamp as automatically fresh information can misdate what a market participant actually learned at that moment.

    Assumption: a historical API endpoint is a complete archive. Failure mode: as the Kalshi documentation itself makes clear, historical partitions are cutoff-governed and object-specific; a satisfied query is not proof of completeness, and account-level fill records are not a substitute for every trader's order history.

    Assumption: a fixed polling interval has no gaps. Failure mode: network failures, rate limiting, and provider outages create irregular gaps even under a nominally fixed schedule; only an explicit gap-detection check, comparing actual elapsed time between successful snapshots to the intended cadence, catches this.

    Assumption: a current-vintage series can be sliced by date to imitate a historical vintage. Failure mode: any revision that occurred after the fact is invisibly baked into every date-sliced value, silently granting a simulated historical decision access to information from its own future.

    Assumption: label availability implies settlement, or the reverse. Failure mode: either overstating how quickly capital would actually be freed, or understating how early an economically meaningful outcome could have informed a subsequent decision.

    Exercises and solution sketches¶

    Exercise 1: ordering five clocks¶

    An event occurs at 12:00 UTC. A wire report about it is published at 12:05 UTC. Your system actually logs receipt of that report at 12:09 UTC. Your process commits to a forecast at 12:10 UTC. The outcome becomes publicly knowable at 12:30 UTC, but the venue does not release funds until 09:00 UTC the next day. Using the available rule from Section 8.5, was this decision's feature usable at decision time, and is the position settled by the time the label is available?

    Solution sketch. The later of publication (12:05) and observation (12:09) is 12:09, which is before the 12:10 decision cutoff, so the feature is usable. The label becomes available at 12:30, well before the next day's 09:00 settlement; the outcome is knowable roughly twenty hours before funds are actually released, illustrating that label availability and settlement are genuinely different clocks here.

    Exercise 2: a revision at the threshold¶

    A monthly series' first-released value for March is 100.02 and for April is 100.00, so the naively coded "did April exceed March" label is initially NO. A later quality-control revision changes April's value to 100.05. Explain the leakage risk of using the revised value in a study whose stated decision cutoff is one day after the initial April release.

    Solution sketch. If the target is the first-released April increase, replacing its NO label with the revised YES changes the target and is incorrect. If the predeclared target is instead the eventual revised increase, that later YES can legitimately be used for evaluation once available; predicting an unknown future label is the point of forecasting. In either design, the revised value cannot enter features, fitting, or calibration before its actual availability. Record both the target's vintage definition and the model's fitting cutoff rather than imposing the impossible requirement that every evaluation outcome already be known at prediction time.

    Exercise 3: pagination and silent selection¶

    A collector requests one page of 100 historical contract records, receives a cursor for further pages, and stops there because 100 records "look like enough." List two concrete ways this could bias a subsequent analysis.

    Solution sketch. If the provider returns records in a systematic order, for example by creation time or by identifier, stopping at one page could concentrate the sample in a narrow slice of the intended date range, silently excluding a large share of the true population. It could also correlate with an unintended property of the excluded majority, such as more recently created or more recently modified contracts being pushed to later pages, biasing any temporal or maturity-related feature computed only from the retained page.

    Exercise 4: a gap in the order book¶

    Two consecutive successfully recorded book snapshots for one contract are 47 minutes apart, though the intended polling cadence is 5 minutes. A resting bid visible in the first snapshot is absent from the second. What must a disciplined pipeline record, and what must it refuse to claim?

    Solution sketch. It must flag the 47-minute interval as a gap relative to the 5-minute intended cadence and record that flag alongside both snapshots. It must refuse to claim that the missing bid was filled; cancellation, a partial fill followed by cancellation, and a full fill are all consistent with its disappearance, and only an independent trade or fill feed, not the order book alone, could distinguish them.

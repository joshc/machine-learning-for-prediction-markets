    29. Run a cautious market-making experiment¶

    Prerequisites¶

    You should be comfortable with the market-making and adverse-selection material of Chapter 21, the bankroll and portfolio-survival discussion of Chapter 22, and the paper-operations machinery from Chapter 24, particularly PaperBroker.synthetic_fill, PaperBroker.mark, and RiskBudget. You should already know why a resting order's touch by the market is not evidence of a fill, and why an unrealized mark is not the same thing as a realized profit.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to run the market-making quickstart, reproduce its marked-loss arithmetic by hand, explain precisely why the script's second, more attractive-looking bid is still rejected, and state what real data, beyond anything this script or its companion package can supply, would be required before treating any of this as evidence about a live maker strategy. You should also be able to explain the difference between an inferred fill and a supplied synthetic fill, and why this distinction matters more in market making than anywhere else in the book.

    29.1 A concrete goal: quoting, filling, and marking a position, then stopping¶

    Market making means posting resting orders on both sides of a market and hoping to earn the spread between them repeatedly. It also means holding inventory whenever one side fills before the other, and inventory carries the same directional risk as any other open position. This quickstart deliberately shows a market maker's least glamorous moment: a one-sided fill, an unfavorable mark, and a stopped second order, rather than a completed favorable round trip.

    Run the script from the companion project root (book\companion):

    Command working directory: run this chapter's Python commands from book\companion. From the trading repository root, run Set-Location book\companion once first. The .venv belongs to that companion project; see companion setup.

    .\.venv\Scripts\python.exe examples\quickstart_market_making.py

    Open examples/quickstart_market_making.py alongside this chapter. It creates a PaperBroker with USD 10.00 cash and the fixture's flat USD 0.01 per-unit fee, submits a resting buy order for 10 YES units at a 0.40 limit, and then explicitly supplies a synthetic fill for all 10 units at that price, with the evidence string "invented one-sided flow".

    29.2 Reading the actual output¶

    The observed run includes the following excerpt (some wrapper and explanatory fields are omitted):

    {
  "result": {
    "filled_buy_units": 10, "filled_sell_units": 0,
    "inventory_liquidation_mark": {
      "cash": "5.90", "inventory_liquidation_value": "2.40",
      "equity": "8.30", "realized_profit": "0",
      "marked_profit": "-1.70", "unrealized_profit": "-1.70"
    },
    "additional_bid_budget_check": {
      "status": "NO_TRADE", "quantity": 0,
      "executable_cost": "4.10", "modeled_expected_profit": "1.90",
      "reason": "cash/reservation or loss budget fails"
    },
    "stop_reason": "inventory concentration and one-sided fills outweigh a displayed spread",
    "evidence_gate": {"status": "REJECT_LIVE_EDGE_INFERENCE"}
  }
}

    Trace the cash and inventory arithmetic from the start. Buying 10 units at 0.40 with a USD 0.01 fee costs \(10\times(0.40+0.01)=4.10\); starting cash of 10.00 minus that cost leaves cash of 5.90, exactly matching the printed cash field inside the mark. The broker now holds 10 units of inventory at a cost basis of 4.10 and has not sold anything, so filled_sell_units is 0 and realized_profit is exactly "0", not an approximation: no sale, and therefore no realized gain or loss, has actually occurred yet.

    The script then marks the position against a deliberately unfavorable new book, snapshot="inventory-stress", quoting bid 0.25 and ask 0.28. PaperBroker.mark computes a conservative full-liquidation value using only the bid side, net of the same exit fee: \(10\times(0.25-0.01)=2.40\), matching inventory_liquidation_value. Equity is cash plus this liquidation value, \(5.90+2.40=8.30\). Marked profit is equity minus the broker's initial cash, \(8.30-10.00=-1.70\), and because realized profit is still zero, unrealized profit equals the same \(-1.70\). This is a mark, not a sale: the ten units are still held, no exit order has actually been submitted, and the -1.70 figure describes what liquidating right now, at this specific hypothetical bid, would produce, not a number that has actually hit the broker's cash balance.

    29.3 Why the additional bid is rejected despite a positive modeled edge¶

    The script's second decision is the one worth studying most closely, because it is not rejected for the same reason as Chapter 27's no-trade outcomes. additional_bid_budget_check screens a fresh PaperInputs with model probability 0.60 against the fixture's default book, ask 0.40. The cost of buying 10 more units is again \(10\times(0.40+0.01)=4.10\), and the modeled expected profit is \(10\times0.60-4.10=6.00-4.10=1.90\), a clearly positive number. If this were the only consideration, the trade would look attractive.

    screen_buy_yes does not stop at that comparison. It also checks budget.permits(cost, available_cash, committed_cash) against the RiskBudget("5.00", "5.00") supplied by the script. After the first fill, the broker's open cost basis is already 4.10 and its reserved cash for any still-open orders is zero, so committed cash going into this second check is 4.10. Adding the new order's cost of 4.10 gives a combined committed total of \(4.10+4.10=8.20\), which exceeds the budget's max_committed_cash of 5.00. The screen therefore returns NO_TRADE with reason "cash/reservation or loss budget fails", even though the same screen's own expected-value comparison was favorable. This is the central lesson of the chapter: a positive modeled edge is a necessary condition for a candidate trade, never a sufficient one, and a risk budget exists precisely to stop a trader from concentrating too much capital in one direction even when each individual step looks attractive in isolation.

    stop_reason names this directly: "inventory concentration and one-sided fills outweigh a displayed spread". The broker already holds a full ten-unit position acquired through a one-sided fill; adding another ten-unit buy would not diversify that exposure, it would double it, in the same direction, against a market that has just shown a materially worse marked value than the entry price.

    29.4 How this connects to bankroll discipline, not just this one screen¶

    RiskBudget.max_committed_cash caps outstanding cost basis and reservations, regardless of an individual candidate's expected value. Multiple positions can have an unaffordable combined worst case even when independent; correlation changes the probability of joint outcomes, not whether the cash limit matters. Fractional Kelly is one optional sizing framework under stronger assumptions, not a necessary tool for every responsible system. This script uses the hard commitment cap, not a Kelly optimizer, and it does not infer an optimal stake from the market's unfavorable move.

    29.5 No proof of a touch fill, and what a supplied synthetic fill actually means¶

    Read PaperBroker.observe_resting in accounting.py: calling it on a resting order against a book that merely touches the order's limit price returns the fixed string "NO_INFERRED_FILL: quotes alone do not establish queue position or a trade", and changes nothing about the order's state. This script does not call observe_resting at all; instead it calls synthetic_fill, which requires an explicit, non-empty evidence string, in this case "invented one-sided flow", and raises ValueError if that string is blank. The distinction matters. A real resting limit order's fill depends on queue priority ahead of it at the same price, the exact sequence of incoming market orders, cancellations by other participants, and latency between an event and the trader's own order reaching the venue, none of which this fixture observes or models. synthetic_fill is a deliberately manual escape hatch for constructing a teaching scenario; it is not a claim that any real resting order would have filled under comparable conditions, and the required evidence string exists specifically so a reader cannot silently invoke it without stating, in writing, what assumption is being made.

    This quickstart shows one supplied fill, an unfavorable mark, and a budget-blocked follow-up. It does not show a completed two-sided round trip; the separate market_making.py demonstration does supply a synthetic favorable round trip realizing USD 0.40. Neither script estimates an empirical fill-conditioned forecast or queue-position model. Supplied scenario fills demonstrate accounting, not the frequency or profitability of those paths in a real market.

    29.6 What real data would be required before treating any of this as live evidence¶

    A defensible empirical maker study needs fill and queue evidence, fill-conditioned outcome forecasts, cancellation latency, and current fees that this script does not supply. Glosten-Milgrom and Kyle offer qualified stock-market mechanisms for informative order flow, not measured fill probabilities for these contracts. GLOSTEN1985 KYLE1985

    Avellaneda-Stoikov's inspected equations 29-30 are approximate quote formulas under arithmetic Brownian stock prices, exponential utility, and Poisson arrivals. Its comparisons include simulated fills and terminal midpoint valuation, not historical executable returns. AVELLANEDA2008 The source therefore motivates inventory-risk questions but does not validate this quickstart's stopping rule or supply a binary-payout execution model. The actual venue/product fee schedule also needs independent checking; the classroom USD 0.01 charge is not that schedule. KALSHI_ROUNDING POLYMARKET_FEES

    None of that data is supplied by the book's educational companion. The market_data module, whose exact commands, four allowlisted public-GET endpoints, and native-field schemas are documented in data/README.md and the examples README, is a complete but strictly optional, explicitly gated, read-only public-snapshot collector: every call requires --acknowledge-terms, it is not connected to PaperBroker, and it acquires only current market metadata and current order-book snapshots, never historical fill-rate, queue, or latency records. It is not a live broker, not a historical archive, and the companion ships no acquired venue payload; running it is a separate action a reader must take, and its output is a new, gitignored .snapshot.json file, never a committed dataset. Building the genuine market-making study this section describes remains a real, unimplemented, permission-gated future project, honestly labeled as such in the book's data-feasibility assessment, not something this quickstart, the collector, or any other script in the book already delivers.

    29.7 Troubleshooting¶

    If you call synthetic_fill with an empty or whitespace-only evidence string while experimenting, it raises ValueError("an explicitly supplied synthetic fill needs an explanation"); this is intentional friction, not a bug, so that every invented fill in your own experiments carries a written justification. If PaperBroker.mark raises ValueError("insufficient bid depth for full liquidation mark"), your stress-test book does not quote enough bid-side depth to liquidate the full held position; either supply more depth or treat the resulting error as informative about a real liquidity constraint rather than something to silently suppress. If your local risk-budget experiment unexpectedly permits a trade you expected to be blocked, double-check that you are passing the broker's actual reconcile()-derived open_cost_basis plus reserved_cash as the committed-cash argument, not zero; a budget check performed against the wrong committed-cash baseline will pass trades it should not.

    29.8 Exercises and solution sketches¶

    Exercise 1: recomputing the mark at a different stress bid¶

    Using the same ten-unit position and USD 0.01 exit fee, recompute the liquidation value, equity, and marked profit if the stress book's bid were 0.35 instead of 0.25.

    Solution sketch. Liquidation value is \(10\times(0.35-0.01)=3.40\). Equity is \(5.90+3.40=9.30\). Marked profit is \(9.30-10.00=-0.70\), still negative because the entry price of 0.40 plus its own 0.01 entry fee exceeds this hypothetical exit bid net of its own exit fee, but noticeably smaller in magnitude than the chapter's -1.70 example.

    Exercise 2: finding the break-even stress bid¶

    At what stress bid would the ten-unit position's marked profit be exactly zero, holding the same USD 0.01 exit fee and the same 4.10 entry cost?

    Solution sketch. Solve \(10\times(b-0.01)-4.10=0\) for the bid \(b\): \(10b-0.10=4.10\), so \(b=0.42\). A bid of 0.42 or higher would produce a nonnegative marked profit under this fee convention; the actual stress book's bid of 0.25 is well below that break-even level, which is exactly why the mark is negative.

    Exercise 3: recomputing the rejected second order at a smaller size¶

    Suppose the additional bid in Section 29.3 were sized at 2 units instead of 10, with the same 0.40 ask, 0.01 fee, and model probability 0.60. Would the risk budget now permit it?

    Solution sketch. Cost is \(2\times(0.40+0.01)=0.82\). Combined committed cash would be \(4.10+0.82=4.92\), which is at or under the budget's max_committed_cash of 5.00, and the order's own cost of 0.82 is under max_order_cost of 5.00 and under available cash. The modeled expected profit, \(2\times0.60-0.82=0.38\), remains positive. At this smaller size, the budget check would likely permit the trade, illustrating that the earlier rejection was specifically a sizing and concentration problem, not a rejection of the underlying model-conditional edge itself.

    Exercise 4: distinguishing a touch from a fill in your own words¶

    A resting bid at 0.38 is posted, and a later book snapshot shows the best available ask at exactly 0.38. Explain why observe_resting still returns NO_INFERRED_FILL in this situation, and name one concrete piece of missing information that would be needed to justify treating this as an actual fill.

    Solution sketch. A matching price alone does not establish that any counterparty actually traded at that price against this specific resting order, nor where this order stood in the queue relative to any other resting orders at the same price. A genuine fill claim would need an actual trade record, or an authoritative venue confirmation, identifying the executed quantity, price, and timestamp against this order specifically, not merely a coincidence between a resting limit price and a later quoted level.

    Exercise 5: a bankroll-level view of the same decision¶

    Using the reasoning from Section 29.4, explain why a trader who tracks only per-trade expected value, and never a running total of committed cash across all currently open positions, could eventually violate their own affordable-loss limit even if every individual trade they accepted looked favorable in isolation.

    Solution sketch. Per-trade expected value is computed one decision at a time and says nothing about how much capital is simultaneously at risk across every position still open at once. A trader who accepts a sequence of individually positive-expected-value trades without tracking cumulative committed cash can end up with a combined exposure, especially under correlated outcomes, that exceeds what they could actually afford to lose, even though no single decision along the way was individually unreasonable. This is exactly the failure mode RiskBudget.max_committed_cash is designed to prevent by checking the running total, not just the newest trade's own arithmetic.

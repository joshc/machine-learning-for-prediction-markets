    4. What a market price can tell you¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Chapters 1-3 (contracts versus payouts, probability and Bayes' rule, expected value with executable costs). Comfort with the idea that an average combines information from multiple sources, and with basic sampling intuition (that repeated correlated observations are not the same as repeated independent observations).

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to: explain the economic mechanism by which a market price can aggregate dispersed information; state precisely what conditions are required for a price to approximate a mean belief, and why "price equals objective probability" is a stronger and generally unsupported claim; describe one real empirical comparison between a prediction market and an alternative forecasting method, together with its stated sample limitations; and avoid three specific inferential mistakes that this literature warns against.

    4.1 The economic intuition: why aggregation can work at all¶

    Before any citation or formula, it is worth building the mechanism in plain terms. Suppose one hundred people each hold a noisy, independent piece of information relevant to whether some proposition is true, and each person is willing to trade an event contract up to the price at which their own private assessment says the trade stops being profitable for them. A person who believes the true probability is 0.70 is willing to buy YES up to a price near 0.70 and would sell if the price rose above roughly 0.70; a person who believes it is 0.30 behaves the mirror image. As these hundred people trade against each other, the price tends to settle somewhere that reflects a weighted blend of their individual assessments, because anyone who thinks the current price is wrong relative to their own belief has a direct financial incentive to trade it toward their belief, and that trading pressure only stops (in a stylized, frictionless picture) when the marginal participant is indifferent.

    This is the essential mechanism behind calling a price an aggregator of dispersed information: it is not that the price "knows" the truth, but that it reflects the net effect of many participants each acting on their own partial information, weighted (in general) by how much they are each willing to risk. That weighting-by-willingness-to-trade is precisely where the mechanism gets complicated, and where the careful theoretical literature earns its keep, because willingness to trade depends on more than just belief — it depends on risk tolerance, available capital, and the cost of being wrong, none of which are the same thing as "how correct someone is."

    4.2 Price as an approximation to a mean belief, not an oracle¶

    Wolfers and Zitzewitz's analysis of interpreting prediction-market prices as probabilities gives sufficient conditions under which a price is closely related to the mean belief of market participants, while also showing that this relationship is an approximation whose quality depends on assumptions such as participants' risk attitudes and the shape of the distribution of their beliefs; outside those conditions the approximation can be biased in a specific, analyzable direction, not merely "less accurate" in a vague sense. WZ2006 The precise, load-bearing phrase here is mean belief, not objective truth. A price can faithfully reflect what a group of participants collectively believes while that collective belief is still wrong, if the group shares a common blind spot, is working from stale information, or is dominated by a small number of large, risk-tolerant participants whose private views happen not to represent the wider available evidence.

    Manski's identification analysis sharpens this further and, in some ways, makes it more restrictive. Under a specified model with risk-neutral, price-taking traders holding heterogeneous beliefs, the equilibrium price only partially identifies the central tendency of those beliefs, and does not identify their dispersion at all. MANSKI2004 In plain terms: even granting a fairly favorable set of modeling assumptions, a single scalar price cannot, by construction, tell you both "what most people think" and "how much people disagree" — two pieces of information that a raw price necessarily collapses into one number. This is not a claim that markets fail to aggregate information; it is a precise statement about what a single equilibrium price can and cannot identify even in a model built to be friendly to the aggregation story. A reader who wants a dispersion estimate needs additional structure — for example, several related contracts, a full distribution of prices across different thresholds of the same underlying quantity, or an entirely different data source — not just one price.

    Taken together, these two results support a specific, carefully bounded claim used throughout this book: a price approximately reflects a mean belief under stated conditions; it is not, without further argument, the true objective probability of the underlying event. The gap between "approximates a mean belief" and "equals the truth" is exactly where naive interpretations of prediction markets go wrong, and it is why this book consistently writes \(p\) as a probability under a stated model or information set rather than as a mysterious ground truth that a price reveals.

    4.3 An actual empirical comparison, and what its numbers do and do not support¶

    Theoretical identification results tell you what is possible in principle; empirical work tells you what has actually been observed in a specific historical setting. Berg, Nelson, and Rietz compared the Iowa Electronic Markets' contemporaneous vote-share forecasts against 964 national polls spanning five United States presidential elections from 1988 through 2004, and reported that the market's forecast was closer to the eventual two-major-party vote split than the polls in 74 percent of those comparisons. BERG2008

    This is a genuine, specific, real result, and it deserves to be stated precisely rather than either dismissed or oversold. Four qualifications, all directly tied to the source's own stated scope, must accompany it.

    First, 964 polls are not 964 independent elections. The underlying reference-class problem is exactly the one raised in Chapter 2: if a large share of those 964 polls are drawn from a small number of election cycles — here, five — then many of the individual poll comparisons share a common outcome and common macro-level drivers (the state of the economy, the incumbent's approval, the specific candidates running that year). Treating 964 as if it represented 964 separately drawn, independent tests of "market versus polls" substantially overstates the statistical weight of this evidence.

    To make the shrinkage concrete without pretending to reproduce the source's own internal statistics: suppose, purely as an illustrative assumption and not a measured figure, that polls within a single election cycle are equicorrelated with correlation \(\rho\), and there are on average \(n\approx964/5\approx193\) polls per cycle. The variance of the mean of \(n\) equicorrelated observations, each with variance \(\sigma^2\), is \(\sigma^2(1+(n-1)\rho)/n\), so the effective sample size — the number of independent observations that would produce the same variance — is

    \[ n_{\text{eff}}=\frac{n}{1+(n-1)\rho}. \]

    At a hypothetical \(\rho=0.5\) (chosen only to illustrate the mechanism, not measured from the source), \(n_{\text{eff}}=193/(1+192\times0.5)=193/97\approx1.99\): under this variance model, the 193 within-cycle measurements have the same mean variance as roughly two independent measurements. Five independent, equally sized cycles would give roughly ten such effective measurements, not 964. This is not an effective-sample-size estimate for Berg and colleagues' analysis: actual cycle sizes, correlations, and the quantity being averaged matter. The directly established count is five election outcomes behind the 964 comparisons.

    Second, the estimand is vote share, not winner-take-all probability. The comparison is about how close each forecasting method came to the actual percentage of the two-party vote, not about how well either method predicted the probability that a given candidate would win. These are different targets: a vote-share forecast of 52% for the incumbent is a point prediction on a continuous scale, while a winner-probability forecast of 0.90 is a probabilistic statement about a binary outcome, and the same underlying data can produce a good vote-share forecast alongside a poorly calibrated win-probability forecast or vice versa. A reader should not silently substitute "the market predicts winners better than polls" for what the source actually measured.

    Third, five elections is a small and selected setting. All five cycles come from one country's presidential elections, using one specific market (the Iowa Electronic Markets), over a bounded historical period. Whether the same relationship holds for legislative races, other countries, other time periods, or markets with different participant compositions, liquidity, and rules is a separate empirical question that this comparison does not answer.

    Fourth, forecast accuracy does not establish post-cost trading profit. The comparison concerns which forecasting method landed closer to the eventual vote share; it says nothing about whether a trading strategy built on that market's prices would have cleared executable costs, capital lockup, and risk constraints of the kind developed in Chapter 3. A closer forecast is not the same claim as a profitable trade, and this source should not be cited as evidence for the latter.

    It is also worth noting, as a small point about intellectual honesty in sourcing, that the institutional page hosting this abstract lists a different page range than the publisher-deposited bibliographic record for the same article; the source ledger discloses that discrepancy rather than silently picking one number, the same standard of care this chapter asks you to apply to the 964-versus-five-elections distinction.

    4.4 A broader empirical synthesis, with the same caution¶

    Wolfers and Zitzewitz's separate 2004 survey assembles evidence on information aggregation and predictive accuracy across several prediction-market contexts, beyond the single election-focused comparison above. WZ2004 It is valuable as a synthesis spanning multiple settings, but it inherits the limitations of the underlying studies it draws on, and it does not, by itself, distinguish correlation from causation in every surveyed context: a market being associated with accurate outcomes in a historical episode does not establish that the market's structure caused the accuracy, as opposed to both being driven by the same information environment. Read this source, like the comparison above, as specific historical findings bounded by their own settings, not a general law that transfers automatically to a different venue, contract type, or era.

    4.5 Assumptions, counterexamples, and failure modes¶

    Assumption: the relevant trader-behavior model applies. Manski's cited model assumes risk-neutral, price-taking traders; Wolfers and Zitzewitz instead discuss how risk preferences and the belief distribution affect price interpretation. Risk neutrality is not a shared prerequisite of both results. A market dominated by a few large or strategically motivated participants can depart from a proposed aggregation model; identify which assumptions fail rather than treating any risk aversion as disqualifying.

    Assumption: the market is liquid and current. A thin, stale, or recently reopened market's price reflects whatever small amount of trading has occurred, which may not represent a meaningful aggregate of anything. Applying the "price approximates mean belief" reasoning to an illiquid market with a handful of trades is exactly the kind of context the underlying theory does not cover.

    Counterexample: a well-aggregated price that is still wrong. If every participant in a market shares access to the same incomplete public information set and no one holds superior private information, the price can faithfully aggregate that shared, incomplete view — and still be wrong once withheld or not-yet-public information is later revealed. Good aggregation of available information is not the same as omniscience about future information.

    Failure mode: treating market accuracy in one historical episode as a permanent property. The empirical results in Sections 4.3 and 4.4 describe specific historical settings. A prediction market operating today, on a different platform, with different participants, contract designs, and liquidity, is not automatically covered by a result observed in 1988-2004 U.S. presidential politics.

    Failure mode: inferring a trading strategy from a forecasting-accuracy result. As stated in Section 4.3, "the market's price was closer to the truth than the polls" is not the same claim as "buying at the market's price and holding to settlement would have been profitable after costs," and the two should never be conflated when writing up research.

    4.6 A worked illustration of aggregation and its limits¶

    Consider three hypothetical forecasters (not real market participants) who separately assess a proposition's probability at 0.30, 0.50, and 0.70. If we explicitly choose an equal-weight linear opinion pool, the result is \((0.30+0.50+0.70)/3=0.50\). This illustrates combining assessments, not a theorem about market clearing: equal capital and distinct information alone do not imply that an actual equilibrium price equals the arithmetic average.

    Now suppose a fourth participant enters holding the same information as the second (0.50) but expresses it as two separately sized trades rather than one, effectively appearing twice in the record of "who traded at what price." If a market microstructure analysis naively treats each trade as an independent unit of evidence, it will overweight this duplicated view relative to the two forecasters holding genuinely distinct information — precisely the dependence problem from Section 4.3's discussion of correlated poll readings, now recast as a market-microstructure version of the same mistake. The remedy in both cases is the same: identify the actual number of independent information sources behind an aggregate figure before treating that figure's apparent precision at face value.

    4.7 Companion example¶

    The companion script bayes_scoring.py, introduced in Chapter 2 for Bayesian updating and proper scoring, also reports calibration bins that group a set of forecasts by predicted probability and compare each bin's mean forecast to its observed outcome rate. That mechanism is the same statistical tool this chapter uses conceptually to ask whether a market's implied probabilities, taken as forecasts, are well-calibrated against realized outcomes over many markets — a question that requires a genuinely large, non-duplicated, and appropriately time-ordered sample to answer credibly, for exactly the dependence reasons discussed above.     Exercises¶

    Exercise 4.1. A dataset contains 500 rows of market-price snapshots for a single event contract, taken every ten minutes over the final two days before settlement. A colleague proposes treating these 500 rows as 500 independent tests of "does the market price predict the outcome." Explain what is wrong with this proposal, connecting it explicitly to Section 4.3's discussion of the 964-poll comparison.

    Solution sketch. All 500 snapshots share exactly one eventual settlement outcome; they are highly autocorrelated repeated observations of a single underlying belief process converging toward one binary resolution, not 500 independent draws. This mirrors the caution that 964 polls drawn from five elections are not 964 independent elections: the correct unit of independent evidence here is closer to "one event," and a rigorous evaluation would need many distinct events, not many snapshots of one.

    Exercise 4.2. Explain, in your own words, why Manski's identification result implies that a single market price cannot simultaneously tell you "what most participants believe" and "how much they disagree," and why this matters for a researcher trying to build a confidence interval around a market-implied probability.

    Solution sketch. A price is one number, produced by aggregate trading behavior; under the stated risk-neutral, price-taking, heterogeneous-belief model, that one number is shown to only partially reveal the central tendency of participants' beliefs and not to reveal their dispersion at all, since dispersion is a separate feature of the belief distribution that does not have to leave a distinguishable mark on the single equilibrium price. A researcher wanting an uncertainty band around a market-implied probability therefore cannot construct one from the price alone; they need an explicit model of belief dispersion or additional data (such as multiple related contracts) that the raw price does not supply by itself.

    Exercise 4.3. A vote-share forecast of 51.5% for a candidate is reported alongside a separate binary win-probability forecast of 0.62 for the same candidate in the same race. Explain why these two numbers are not contradictory even though 51.5% sounds like "barely over half" while 0.62 sounds like "clearly favored."

    Solution sketch. The two forecasts answer different questions: the vote-share forecast is a point estimate on a continuous scale describing the expected proportion of votes, while the win-probability forecast describes the probability of crossing whatever threshold determines victory (which may not simply be 50%, depending on the electoral mechanism), incorporating the uncertainty around that point estimate. A close point estimate on the vote share can still correspond to a substantially favored win probability if the estimated uncertainty around that point estimate is small relative to the margin, since even a modest lead becomes a strong favorite once its estimated variability is tight enough.

    Exercise 4.4. A research report claims: "because our market-derived forecasts were closer to actual outcomes than a competing poll-based method in a historical backtest, a trading strategy based on buying whichever side the market forecast favored would have been profitable." Identify the specific inferential gap in this claim using Section 4.3's fourth qualification.

    Solution sketch. Forecast accuracy (closeness to the eventual outcome) and trading profitability are different estimands; the claim silently substitutes evidence about the former for a conclusion about the latter without ever introducing executable prices, fees, capital lockup, or a risk budget, any of which could turn an accurate-looking forecast into an unprofitable trade once real costs and constraints from Chapter 3 are applied.

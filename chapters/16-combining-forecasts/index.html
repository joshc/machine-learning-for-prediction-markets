    16. Combining and maintaining forecasts¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Read Chapters 11–15 before this one; this chapter treats the logistic baseline, boosted-and-calibrated model, TF-IDF model, and small neural network built there as a set of forecasters to be combined, not as competitors from which you must pick exactly one winner. You should be comfortable with expectations, variance, and simple single-variable calculus (finding a minimum by setting a derivative to zero). The notation registry applies throughout, and its warning that mixing calibration with averaged predictions can change calibration is the organizing claim of this chapter, derived in full below rather than merely asserted.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to: explain why majority voting is inappropriate for combining probability forecasts and derive a proper alternative; construct and verify a concrete example showing that averaging two individually calibrated forecasters does not, in general, preserve calibration; derive the minimum-variance linear combination weight for two forecasters from first principles; explain why a combined model still needs its own held-out calibration step, using genuinely unused data; and explain how model dependence, drift, and abstention change what a responsible combination system reports, using the companion's own fixed-blend result as a concrete cautionary example.

    16.1 Several imperfect forecasters, and the temptation to just vote¶

    By Chapter 15 there are six candidate forecasts for the fixture: a base rate, invented market benchmark, logistic model, calibrated boosted model, TF-IDF model, and small neural network, with raw boosting also available as a diagnostic. No uniform winner has been established. Chapter 12's calibrator was fitted on validation and scored on test, where it worsened the raw model's score; Chapter 15's neural advantage is a few thousandths on one run. This chapter asks how to combine candidates without treating that small comparison as a definitive ranking.

    A natural but wrong instinct, borrowed from classification-accuracy thinking, is to have each model vote (YES or NO) and take the majority. This discards exactly the information a proper scoring rule (Chapter 10) rewards you for reporting: a model that is 51% confident and a model that is 99% confident both cast the same single vote, even though they are making very different claims. Combining probability forecasts needs a method that operates on the probabilities themselves, not on a discarded binary summary of them.

    16.2 Linear pooling, and forecast combination as a genuine research question¶

    The simplest probabilistic combination is a linear pool: a weighted average of the individual forecasts, \(\hat p_{\text{combined}} = \sum_k w_k\hat p_k\) with \(w_k\ge0\) and \(\sum_k w_k=1\). Forecast combination has its own dedicated literature. BATES1969's inspected publisher abstract reports a concrete empirical case — combining two independently produced sets of airline-passenger point forecasts — where the composite forecast achieved lower mean-square error than either original set, and it describes estimating combination weights from each forecaster's past errors rather than fixing them by hand. Read that result precisely: it is evidence that combining point forecasts of a continuous quantity can sometimes reduce mean-square error, and it explicitly describes weight estimation, consistent with this chapter's insistence (Section 16.4) that weights be fit from data, not simply asserted. It is not a theorem that combining probability forecasts of a binary event preserves calibration — a distinct question this book's own scoring conventions care about, and precisely the question Section 16.3 below answers by direct construction rather than by appeal to this or any other citation. The weight derivation in Section 16.4 is produced independently of BATES1969's specific airline-forecast setting. GNEITING2013 studies linear and nonlinear combinations of full predictive distributions through the lenses of coherence, calibration, and dispersion, with simulation and case studies in its abstract; its central, transferable warning — that combining calibrated components does not automatically yield a calibrated combination — is exactly what Section 16.3 demonstrates concretely below. YAO2018 extends stacking (a data-driven combination method, introduced generally in Section 16.4) to full Bayesian predictive distributions, comparing it with Bayesian model averaging in what its abstract calls an "M-open" setting, where no single candidate model is assumed to be the true data-generating process — a more realistic assumption for prediction-market forecasting than assuming one of your six models is exactly correct.

    16.3 A worked demonstration: averaging can break calibration¶

    Notation.md warns that mixing calibration with averaged predictions can change calibration, and asks that this not be assumed to be preserved without explanation. Here is the explanation, built from a deliberately extreme but fully explicit and verifiable construction, chosen specifically to isolate the algebra rather than to resemble a realistic forecaster.

    Consider 100 events, 50 resolving YES and 50 resolving NO. Forecaster A always predicts \(\hat p_A=0.5\), regardless of the event. Among the 100 events where A predicted 0.5, the observed YES rate is exactly \(50/100=0.5\) — A is perfectly calibrated (trivially, since it only ever issues one prediction). Forecaster B is constructed, for this demonstration only, as an oracle: it predicts \(\hat p_B=1.0\) for every event that will resolve YES and \(\hat p_B=0.0\) for every event that will resolve NO. Among the 50 events where B predicted 1.0, the observed rate is \(100\%\); among the 50 events where B predicted 0.0, the observed rate is \(0\%\) — B is also, trivially, perfectly calibrated at each of its two predicted values.

    Now average them, equally weighted: for a YES event, \(\hat p_{\text{avg}} = (0.5+1.0)/2 = 0.75\); for a NO event, \(\hat p_{\text{avg}} = (0.5+0.0)/2 = 0.25\). Check calibration of the average directly. Among the 50 events where the average predicted exactly 0.75, every single one resolved YES — the observed rate is \(100\%\), not \(75\%\). Among the 50 events where the average predicted exactly 0.25, every single one resolved NO — the observed rate is \(0\%\), not \(25\%\) (both counts checked with a script). The average of two perfectly calibrated forecasters is, in this construction, badly miscalibrated at both of its predicted values.

    The mechanism is not exotic: A and B are looking at the same events through completely different information (A ignores the outcome-relevant information entirely; B, unrealistically, has it exactly), and averaging their outputs mixes two forecasts whose calibration was only ever established within each forecaster's own predicted-value groups, not across the specific pairing produced by averaging. Calibration is a property of a particular mapping from predicted value to observed frequency; changing the mapping (by taking an average) can and generally will change which predicted values correspond to which observed frequencies, even when every input to the average was itself calibrated. The realistic version of this same effect, with two genuinely imperfect (not oracle) forecasters whose information overlaps only partially, is milder in degree but identical in mechanism, which is exactly why this book does not let you assume a combined model is calibrated without checking it directly, exactly as you would check any other model.

    16.4 Learned combination weights, derived from first principles¶

    Rather than fixing weights by hand (as the equal 0.5/0.5 average above), a stacking or combination-weight-fitting approach chooses weights that minimize a stated loss on data the weights were not used to produce. The simplest fully worked case is two forecasters combined to minimize expected squared error (Brier score, restricted to this two-forecaster linear-pool case).

    Let \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) be forecast-minus-outcome errors with variances \(v_1\), \(v_2\) and covariance \(c\). For now assume both errors have mean zero, so variance equals mean squared error. Without this assumption the following calculation minimizes error variance, not necessarily Brier score. The combined error is \(w e_1+(1-w)e_2\), with variance

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(e_{\text{combined}}) = w^2 v_1 + (1-w)^2 v_2 + 2w(1-w)\,c. \]

    Minimizing over \(w\): differentiate with respect to \(w\) and set to zero, \(2wv_1 - 2(1-w)v_2 + 2c(1-2w) = 0\), which rearranges to \(w(v_1+v_2-2c) = v_2-c\), giving

    \[ w^\ast = \frac{v_2-c}{v_1+v_2-2c}. \]

    This is the unconstrained optimum when \(v_1+v_2-2c=\operatorname{Var}(e_1-e_2)>0\). For a convex probability pool, constrain \(w\) to \([0,1]\) by taking the nearest endpoint if the formula lies outside it. If the denominator is zero, the error difference is constant and the variance is independent of weight; under the zero-mean assumption the errors coincide. For possibly biased forecasts, use second moments instead: replace \(v_j\) by \(\mathbb E[e_j^2]\) and \(c\) by \(\mathbb E[e_1e_2]\) to minimize expected Brier loss, again respecting the weight constraint.

    Work a concrete instance: \(v_1=0.09\), \(v_2=0.04\), \(c=0.01\). Then \(w^\ast = (0.04-0.01)/(0.09+0.04-0.02) = 0.03/0.11 = 0.272727\) — weighting the noisier first forecaster (\(v_1=0.09\)) less than the quieter second one (\(v_2=0.04\)), exactly as intuition suggests. The resulting combined variance is \(0.031818\), compared with \(0.037500\) for a naive equal-weight (0.5/0.5) combination on the same two forecasters (both checked with a script) — the fitted weight strictly improves on the naive average here, because it accounts for both forecasters' relative noise and their correlation.

    This derivation assumes \(v_1\), \(v_2\), and \(c\) are known; in practice they must be estimated from data the weight-fitting procedure did not also use to select itself, exactly the same discipline Chapter 12's isotonic calibrator required a separate validation cohort for. Fitting combination weights on the same validation fold already used to calibrate one of the base models, and then evaluating the combination on that same fold, reuses data in exactly the leakage-prone way this book has repeatedly warned against; a genuinely disciplined combination pipeline needs its own held-out fold, distinct from whatever was used to fit or calibrate each base model individually.

    16.5 What the companion's fixed blend actually shows¶

    Run the companion script:

    Command working directory: run this chapter's Python commands from book\companion. From the trading repository root, run Set-Location book\companion once first. The .venv belongs to that companion project; see companion setup.

    .\.venv\Scripts\python.exe examples\forecast_models.py

    Its reported fixed_equal_ensemble is precisely a 0.5/0.5 linear pool of the logistic model and the calibrated boosted model — a fixed blend, not a fitted or learned combination weight, and the script's own naming is honest about this. On the 45 test events, fixed_equal_ensemble scores Brier 0.224694 and log loss 0.636406, compared with the logistic model alone at Brier 0.198372 and the calibrated boosted model alone at Brier 0.267200. The fixed 50/50 blend is worse than the logistic model by itself, and better than the calibrated boosted model by itself, on this run. Report this exactly as it is: naive equal-weight blending here does not beat the best individual component. This is a genuine, disclosed, unfavorable result for the fixed-blend approach on this fixture, not a hidden failure — and it is a direct, concrete illustration of Section 16.4's point that an unfitted weight (0.5/0.5, chosen without reference to either model's actual error variance) can be dominated by simply using the better single model, exactly as the general theory predicts when one component is considerably noisier than the other.

    Moving from this fixed blend to something like the fitted weight of Section 16.4 would require a validation cohort not already spent on fitting the isotonic calibrator, and would need its own separate held-out check before being trusted — this book's companion does not currently implement that additional fold, and the honest lesson here is naming that gap rather than quietly assuming a fitted weight would have done better without checking.

    16.6 Model dependence: why six forecasters are not six independent opinions¶

    All six forecasters discussed in this part of the book are fit, directly or indirectly, from the same three underlying numeric features (or, for the TF-IDF model, from text generated as a near-deterministic function of the same underlying signal) and the same synthetic data-generating process. Their errors are therefore likely to be substantially correlated: on an event where the underlying signal feature happens to be unusually noisy relative to the true outcome, every model that relies on it, directly or through a derived representation, is liable to be misled in a similar direction at once. A combination of \(k\) such correlated forecasters does not behave like a combination of \(k\) genuinely independent opinions; its effective diversity — informally, how much distinct information the ensemble actually contains — is smaller than \(k\) suggests, exactly as Chapter 7's discussion of dependence groups warned that counting rows is not the same as counting independent information.

    This matters for weight fitting. Under the zero-mean assumption, if \(v_1\geq v_2\) and \(c\) rises to \(v_2\), the optimal convex weight on model 1 reaches zero and the pool cannot improve variance on model 2. Perfect positive correlation likewise offers no convex-pooling variance benefit over the less variable component. Unrestricted negative weights can behave differently and need not yield valid probabilities, which is why the constraint matters. Estimate covariance and bias on appropriate held-out predictions instead of treating several models trained on the same information as independent opinions.

    16.7 Drift: a combination is not fit-once-forever¶

    A forecasting relationship that held during a training period can weaken, strengthen, or change direction as the underlying process evolves — a market family's rules change, a recurring event's seasonal pattern shifts, or a regime genuinely breaks. GANIN2016 documents domain-adversarial training, an approach that uses labeled data from one (source) domain together with unlabeled data from a related (target) domain to learn representations that remain useful for the task while becoming less distinguishable between domains; conceptually, this is one documented way researchers have approached the problem of a model trained under one set of conditions needing to remain useful under a related, shifted set of conditions. Its abstract's own limitation applies directly here: this is not a guarantee of successful transfer across market families or across a real regime change, and it is not implemented anywhere in this book's companion.

    Practically, maintaining a forecasting or combination system over time means monitoring calibration and score on a rolling basis as new, genuinely out-of-sample events resolve (reusing Chapter 10's evaluation machinery, not inventing a new one), being willing to detect that a previously reasonable weight or model has stopped performing as it once did (Chapter 13's brief discussion of changepoint detection is one relevant, appropriately hedged tool for this), and retraining on a schedule tied to when new labels genuinely become available, not to whatever cadence is operationally convenient. None of this eliminates the risk of drift; it makes the system's response to drift auditable rather than silent.

    16.8 Uncertainty, disagreement, and the case for abstention¶

    When the base forecasters substantially disagree with each other, that disagreement is itself informative, and treating it as a signal for caution rather than forcing a confident combined point estimate is a legitimate design choice. A Bayesian component's posterior distribution (Chapter 11) can in principle support such a decision by quantifying how much of the disagreement reflects genuine parameter uncertainty within its own model — but, exactly as Chapter 11 warned, a posterior's credible interval is a statement of uncertainty conditional on that model being adequate, not a guarantee against misspecification, and it should not be mistaken for a certified safety margin. A combination system that abstains when its components disagree sharply, or when a component's own uncertainty is wide, is choosing a smaller, better-supported coverage over a larger but less trustworthy one, in the same spirit as Chapter 25's abstention-under-coverage discussion; it is a design decision to be evaluated on its own coverage-versus-score tradeoff, not a way to avoid ever being evaluated.

    16.9 Failure modes¶

    Majority voting discards probability information, and averaging calibrated components need not preserve calibration. Fitting combination weights and evaluating them on the same records also creates selection optimism. Correlated models are not independent opinions.

    A fixed convex blend can score worse than the better component, as the companion demonstrates, but not worse than both components on the same equally weighted cases under Brier or log loss. Each loss is convex in the reported probability, so its loss at the blend is at most the corresponding weighted average of component losses; averaging over the common cases preserves that inequality. This does not guarantee beating the best component or preserving calibration. Finally, posterior width remains conditional on a model, not a certificate against misspecification.

    Exercises and solution sketches¶

    Exercise 1: constructing a calibration-breaking average¶

    Forecaster A always predicts 0.5 on a population with a true 50% base rate; forecaster B predicts 1.0 on every event that resolves YES and 0.0 on every event that resolves NO. Compute the average forecast for a YES event and for a NO event, and state the observed rate among each group of averaged predictions.

    Solution sketch. YES event: \((0.5+1.0)/2=0.75\); among all events predicted 0.75 by the average, 100% resolve YES (not 75%). NO event: \((0.5+0.0)/2=0.25\); among all events predicted 0.25, 0% resolve YES (not 25%). Both individual forecasters are calibrated at their own predicted values; their average is not calibrated at its predicted values.

    Exercise 2: the minimum-variance two-forecaster weight¶

    Two forecasters have error variances \(v_1=0.09\) and \(v_2=0.04\) and error covariance \(c=0.01\). Compute the minimum-variance weight \(w^\ast\) on the first forecaster and the resulting combined variance.

    Solution sketch. \(w^\ast = (v_2-c)/(v_1+v_2-2c) = (0.04-0.01)/(0.09+0.04-0.02) = 0.03/0.11 = 0.272727\). Combined variance: \(w^{\ast2}v_1+(1-w^\ast)^2v_2+2w^\ast(1-w^\ast)c = 0.031818\), less than the equal-weight combination's variance of \(0.0375\).

    Exercise 3: reading the companion's fixed-blend result¶

    The companion reports logistic Brier 0.198372, boosted-calibrated Brier 0.267200, and fixed-equal-ensemble Brier 0.224694 on the same 45 test events. What does this ordering show about naive equal-weight blending here, and what would be needed before concluding a fitted weight would do better?

    Solution sketch. The blend scores worse than logistic and better than calibrated boosting on this sample. A fitted weight needs base-model predictions generated without fitting or calibrating those models on the weight-training outcomes. After weights are selected, improvement must be checked on a further untouched evaluation cohort, not on the same fold used to select the weights. A nested or chronological cross-fitting design can organize those roles, but the companion implements only the fixed blend.

    Exercise 4: correlated forecaster errors¶

    Two forecasters are fit from the same three underlying features and the same data-generating process. Explain why treating their combination as gaining "two independent opinions' worth" of variance reduction would be optimistic, using the minimum-variance formula's covariance term.

    Solution sketch. As the error covariance \(c\) approaches the smaller of \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) (highly correlated errors), the achievable combined variance approaches the smaller individual variance rather than dropping further, so little or no combination benefit remains; assuming independence (implicitly \(c=0\)) when the true covariance is high overstates the variance reduction the combination can actually deliver.

    Exercise 5: abstention as a design choice¶

    A combined forecasting system's base models disagree sharply on a subset of events. Propose one disciplined way to handle this disagreement, and state what evaluation would be needed to check whether the approach actually helps.

    Solution sketch. Abstain from issuing a forecast (or issue a wide interval rather than a point estimate) on events where base-model disagreement exceeds a predeclared threshold, and report performance separately for the covered and abstained populations. This needs to be evaluated by comparing the covered subset's score against a baseline permitted a comparable abstention budget, on held-out data, exactly as Chapter 25's coverage-versus-baseline discussion requires — abstaining is not itself evidence of improved decision quality until checked this way.

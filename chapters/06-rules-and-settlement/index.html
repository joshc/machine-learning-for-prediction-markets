    6. Rules, venues, and settlement risk¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Chapters 1-5, especially Chapter 1's \(Z\)/\(Y\)/\(R\) distinction (informal event, contractual outcome, realized payout) and Chapter 5's order-book mechanics. No new mathematics is introduced; this chapter is about reading and reasoning carefully about documented rules.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to: enumerate the specific documented settlement, void, and exceptional-payout mechanics of Kalshi and Polymarket as inspected in this book's research audit; compute the accounting consequences of a void, fractional, or delayed settlement using the book's canonical position; distinguish collateral, custody, and currency-unit risk from event-probability risk; and identify, rather than paper over, the current unknowns in each venue's documentation, including geographic eligibility.

    6.1 Why this chapter is deliberately narrow and citation-heavy¶

    Every other chapter in this book builds general concepts that should remain true regardless of which specific venue a reader eventually uses. This chapter is different by design: it works through two specific, real venues' documented rules, because settlement risk is not a generic mathematical object you can derive from first principles the way Bayes' rule or the LMSR cost function can be derived. It is a matter of what a specific operator's specific documentation, retrieved on a specific date, actually says. Every claim in this chapter about Kalshi or Polymarket is tied to a named, dated source in the research ledger, and every claim carries the same warning the ledger itself carries: official documentation describes the retrieved page at the time it was inspected, not a permanent contract with future readers, and it can change.

    This chapter's treatment is also explicitly incomplete in scope. It is not marketing material, a comparison ranking, an endorsement, or a complete legal or fee summary for either operator. It is a worked demonstration of the specific documented mechanics that bear on the \(Y\)/\(R\) distinction from Chapter 1, on the accounting conventions used throughout this book, and on the unresolved questions a careful reader should keep asking rather than assume away.

    6.2 Kalshi: settlement, rounding, and the live/historical boundary¶

    Kalshi's documented settlement guide describes ordinary YES/NO contracts paying out according to net-position settlement, with settlement timing that varies by contract rather than being instantaneous or uniform across all markets. KALSHI_SETTLEMENT The same documentation distinguishes zero fees for a simple, ordinary YES/NO determination from the possibility of sub-cent scalar-settlement fees and rounding effects on other contract types, meaning "settlement" is not a single uniform mechanical event across every contract Kalshi lists; a scalar or otherwise non-binary contract can carry settlement mechanics that a purely binary mental model misses entirely. KALSHI_SETTLEMENT

    The separate fee-rounding guide distinguishes trade fee, rounding fee, and rebate, with balance precision depending on membership or clearing type and an accumulator across fills of one order. KALSHI_ROUNDING That differs from both the canonical flat classroom fee and the companion's independent cent rounding per fill. An accumulator may offset rounding that would otherwise accumulate across fragmented fills; its existence alone does not prove that splitting an order raises the final fee. Trace the actual applicable schedule, accumulator, and rebates before drawing that conclusion for a real account.

    Kalshi's historical-data documentation describes a live/historical partition governed by moving cutoff timestamps, retrievable through a documented endpoint, with different object types (for example, individual markets versus broader event or series containers) subject to different partition times; events and series remain available through their original endpoints even as individual market data moves across the cutoff. KALSHI_HISTORY The important limitation, stated plainly in the ledger, is that this endpoint list does not establish that a complete historical order-book archive exists or is retrievable; a researcher should verify what depth of historical order-book state, if any, is actually available for a specific historical window before assuming a full historical order-book replay (Chapter 5's order-book mechanics, applied retrospectively) is possible.

    Finally, Kalshi's own demo environment documentation is worth citing precisely because of what it explicitly disclaims: it provides a mock-funds environment with credentials separate from production, and it explicitly warns that demo prices and behavior may not reflect real markets. KALSHI_DEMO A mechanics test conducted in a demo environment is a legitimate way to learn an API's request and response shapes; it is not evidence about real liquidity, real price behavior, or real profitability, and this book does not treat it as such anywhere.

    6.3 Polymarket: resolution, disputes, and the Unknown outcome¶

    Polymarket's documented resolution process states directly that a contract's specific rules, not its market title, determine settlement, and describes a proposal-and-challenge flow (a proposed outcome, a challenge period during which the proposal can be disputed, and — for disputed cases — a documented vote table) rather than an instantaneous automatic determination. POLYMARKET_RESOLUTION The documentation further describes an "Unknown" exceptional outcome, reached through this dispute process, under which each side's token redeems for USD 0.50 rather than USD 0 or USD 1 — a genuine, documented third state that a purely binary \(Y\in\{0,1\}\) model cannot represent. POLYMARKET_RESOLUTION

    This is directly the fractional-payout case introduced abstractly in Chapter 1 and demonstrated concretely in the companion code, now grounded in an actual documented mechanism at a specific real venue. Consider the canonical classroom position: ten YES units bought at USD 0.40 with the invented USD 0.01 per-unit fee, for an initial cost of USD 4.10. Applying a half-unit payout to that USD example gives total receipts of USD 5.00 and net profit

    \[ 10\times0.50-4.10=5.00-4.10=0.90, \]

    a positive but structurally different result from either ordinary binary outcome (+5.90 or -4.10). It does not establish which side of the original question was correct. Nor does it make the two tokens cease to have a constant combined payout: 0.50 plus 0.50 is still one unit of collateral. Contrast this with a hypothetical full-refund void convention: if an operator returned the entire USD 4.10, including the invented entry fee, net profit would be zero. If only the USD 4.00 purchase notional were refunded, profit would instead be minus USD 0.10. Neither refund convention is established for either venue by the inspected resolution page; "void" alone does not specify the cash flows. A training pipeline must predeclare how ordinary binary outcomes, exceptional fractional payouts, and any actual refund rules are represented rather than rounding them all into binary labels. Section 6.4 separately explains why a venue's collateral payout is not automatically risk-free cash USD.

    Polymarket's documentation additionally describes "additional context" clarifications that can be issued for a market after trading has already begun. POLYMARKET_RESOLUTION This is the concrete, documented instance of the Chapter 1 assumption that "the rules are stable once trading opens" can fail: a clarification issued mid-market changes what a correct forecast needed to account for, and a model trained purely on the earliest version of a market's rules text may be implicitly trained on a slightly different contract than the one that eventually settles.

    The Polymarket markets-and-events documentation, already introduced in Chapters 1 and 5 for its caution about event groupings not automatically being exclusive partitions, adds one further operational detail relevant here: order-book trading additionally requires a specific enableOrderBook property on the market, meaning not every listed market necessarily supports the central-limit-order-book trading model described in Chapter 5 at all. POLYMARKET_MARKETS A researcher assuming uniform order-book access across an entire dataset of listed markets should verify this property rather than assume it.

    6.4 Collateral and custody: pUSD, USDC, and what "USD" means¶

    This book's canonical accounting examples are written in plain USD for pedagogical simplicity. Real collateral mechanics can differ substantially from an implicit assumption of simple, riskless cash. Polymarket's documentation describes its own collateral instrument, referred to as pUSD, as a Polygon-network ERC-20 token representing a claim on underlying USDC, with documented wrapping and unwrapping operations and an operator-controlled pause gate. POLYMARKET_COLLATERAL Separately, Polymarket's fee documentation describes market-specific taker fees that depend on share price and quantity, together with a distinction between fee-free categories and maker-side treatment, rather than a single flat schedule. POLYMARKET_FEES

    Three distinct units appear across this cluster of documentation, and they are not interchangeable: USD, the plain currency unit this book's classroom examples use; USDC, a specific stablecoin that Polymarket's own materials describe pUSD as representing a claim on; and pUSD, the wrapped token that actually moves through the platform's own trading and settlement flows. A trader holding pUSD holds a token whose value is designed to track USDC, which is in turn designed to track the US dollar, but each link in that chain is a distinct claim with its own operational mechanics — wrapping, unwrapping, a pause gate, smart-contract risk, and reliance on the issuing entities behind the stablecoin — rather than a single riskless unit. The operator's own description of these mechanics is exactly that: an operator description, not an independent audit of reserves, contract security, or redemption guarantees, and this book does not treat it as one. A model's probability estimate about an event's outcome says nothing whatsoever about the separate question of whether the collateral backing a position can actually be redeemed at face value when needed.

    Kalshi's documented settlement and fee-rounding materials, by contrast, describe cash balances directly rather than an intermediate wrapped token, but they too specify balance precision that depends on membership or clearing type, meaning "how much cash you actually have, to what precision" is itself governed by documented account-level rules rather than being a universal constant. KALSHI_ROUNDING

    6.5 Changes, latency, and eligibility: what remains genuinely unknown¶

    Two categories of documented limitation deserve to be named directly rather than smoothed over.

    Documentation changes and latency. Every official-documentation source cited in this chapter was retrieved on a single audit date; each source's own status explicitly notes that it describes mutable documentation. KALSHI_SETTLEMENT KALSHI_ROUNDING KALSHI_HISTORY POLYMARKET_RESOLUTION POLYMARKET_COLLATERAL POLYMARKET_FEES A fee schedule, a rounding accumulator, a historical-data cutoff, or a dispute-resolution timeline read today may differ from the same page next month; any implementation relying on these mechanics must be rechecked against current documentation before being trusted, and this book's own citations should be understood as a snapshot, not a permanent specification.

    Eligibility is a genuinely open question, not a resolved one. This book's research audit attempted to inspect Polymarket's terms of use and received only a partial official response: the retrieved page displayed a geographic and product notice stating that trading on the main polymarket.com product was blocked for the United States, pointing instead to a separate polymarket.us product, without the audit successfully retrieving the complete operative terms for either product. POLYMARKET_TERMS This is explicitly recorded in the ledger as a partial official response, not a verified statement of full eligibility rules, current account requirements, or data-reuse permissions for any reader in any jurisdiction. Do not treat this chapter, or any other part of this book, as resolving whether a particular reader in a particular location is currently eligible to use a particular product; that determination requires independently and currently checking the specific operator's specific current terms for the specific product in question, at the time of the reader's own inquiry, not this book's audit-date snapshot.

    6.6 Assumptions, counterexamples, and failure modes¶

    Assumption: settlement is a single instantaneous event. Both Kalshi's variable settlement timing and Polymarket's proposal-challenge-vote flow show that "settled" can be a process with a duration and, in disputed cases, a nontrivial timeline, not an instant. A capital-lockup calculation (Chapter 3) that assumes zero settlement delay can understate how long capital is actually tied up.

    Assumption: a binary label always exists. Section 6.3's Unknown/50-50 case and the void-with-refund case both produce payouts, or absence of payout, that do not correspond to \(Y\in\{0,1\}\). A machine learning pipeline that blindly assigns 0 or 1 to every historical row destroys real information and can introduce systematic label noise concentrated exactly in the contracts that were, by nature, the most contested or ambiguous ones.

    Counterexample: two "identical-sounding" contracts with different rule mechanics. A binary contract on one venue and a superficially similar contract on another venue can have different dispute processes, different exceptional-state payouts, and different collateral instruments; treating them as fungible for a cross-venue arbitrage argument (a topic developed further in Chapter 20) requires verifying all of these, not just comparing headline prices.

    Failure mode: assuming a demo environment measures real liquidity. Kalshi's own documentation disclaims this directly; a strategy validated only against demo-environment behavior has not been validated against real order-book depth, real counterparty behavior, or real fee application.

    Failure mode: treating a wrapped collateral token as risk-free cash. pUSD's documented wrapping and pause-gate mechanics introduce operational and smart-contract risk layers that a plain-USD accounting model, like this book's canonical examples, does not represent; a real position denominated in pUSD carries risk beyond the modeled event probability.

    Failure mode: assuming current eligibility from an old reading of terms. Given the partial and time-stamped nature of the terms-of-use inspection recorded here, any claim of eligibility more than momentarily should be independently rechecked at the time of actual use, not inferred from this chapter's audit-date snapshot.

    6.7 Companion example¶

    The companion script payoffs.py, already used in Chapters 1 and 3, includes exactly the exceptional-fractional settlement path discussed in Section 6.3: it settles the canonical ten-unit position at a payout of USD 0.50 per unit, reports the resulting realized profit and cash balance, and confirms that the resulting binary label is undefined (not 0, not 1) under this book's settlement representation. It also demonstrates that attempting to compute an ordinary binary proper score against that fractional-payout observation is explicitly rejected rather than silently coerced into a binary label. As throughout this book, the settlement is applied to an in-memory paper ledger for illustration; it is not a durable record and it is not a substitute for a venue's own settlement process described in Sections 6.2 and 6.3. Run it from the companion project root (book\companion):

    Command working directory: run this chapter's Python commands from book\companion. From the trading repository root, run Set-Location book\companion once first. The .venv belongs to that companion project; see companion setup.

    .\.venv\Scripts\python.exe examples\payoffs.py

    Compare its reported profit for the exceptional_fractional settlement path against Section 6.3's computed USD 0.90, and note that its binary_ML_label field for that same path is reported as undefined rather than as a rounded 0 or 1 — the direct code-level expression of this chapter's central caution about settlement risk and machine learning labels.

    Exercises¶

    Exercise 6.1. Using the canonical position (10 YES units, USD 0.40 ask, USD 0.01 per-unit fee, USD 4.10 initial cost), compute net profit under: (a) ordinary YES settlement, (b) ordinary NO settlement, (c) the documented Unknown/50-50 exceptional outcome, and (d) a full-refund void. State which, if any, produce a well-defined binary machine-learning label.

    Solution sketch. (a) \(10(1.00)-4.10=5.90\); (b) \(10(0)-4.10=-4.10\); (c) \(10(0.50)-4.10=0.90\); (d) refund of the full USD 4.10 cost basis gives net profit of exactly \(0.00\). Only (a) and (b) produce payouts in \(\{0,1\}\) per unit and therefore a well-defined binary label; (c) and (d) require an explicit non-binary or missing-label representation, not a rounded substitute.

    Exercise 6.2. A researcher builds a training dataset by pulling every historical Kalshi market's final settlement value from a database, without checking each row's source vintage against the documented live/historical cutoff mechanism. Identify one concrete way this could introduce an error, referencing Section 6.2.

    Solution sketch. Because different object types (individual markets versus event or series containers) are documented to move across the live/historical boundary at different times, a naive bulk pull risks mixing records retrieved through different endpoints with different completeness or update guarantees, and does not by itself establish that historical order-book depth (as opposed to final settlement values) is available for the same historical window; a researcher should verify, per object type, which endpoint and cutoff actually governs the specific fields being used, rather than assuming uniform coverage.

    Exercise 6.3. Explain why "pUSD tracks USDC, and USDC tracks the US dollar" is not the same claim as "a position denominated in pUSD carries no risk beyond the underlying event's probability," using the specific documented mechanics from Section 6.4.

    Solution sketch. Each link in that chain (pUSD-to-USDC, USDC-to-USD) is itself a distinct claim resting on separate operational mechanics — Polymarket's own documented wrapping, unwrapping, and pause-gate controls for pUSD, and separately, the reserve and redemption mechanics of the underlying stablecoin issuer for USDC — none of which have been independently audited by this book; a probability estimate about the underlying event says nothing about whether any of these additional mechanical or counterparty links function as intended when a trader actually attempts to redeem value.

    Exercise 6.4. A reader outside the United States asks whether they are eligible to trade on a specific Polymarket product today. What does this chapter's research actually establish about that question, and what should the reader do before concluding they are eligible?

    Solution sketch. This chapter's research establishes only that a retrieved page displayed a notice blocking the main polymarket.com product for United States users and pointing to a separate polymarket.us product, without the audit successfully retrieving either product's complete current operative terms; it establishes nothing about a non-U.S. reader's eligibility for either product. The reader should independently retrieve and read the specific product's current terms of use at the time of their own inquiry, since eligibility rules are exactly the kind of mutable, product- and jurisdiction-specific detail this book's audit-date documentation snapshot cannot settle on their behalf.

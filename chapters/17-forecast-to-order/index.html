    17. The forecast-to-order decision¶

    Prerequisites¶

    You should already be comfortable with conditional probability, the difference between a modeled probability \(\hat p\) and a realized binary outcome \(Y\), executable order-book prices from the market-microstructure material, and the book's canonical accounting example from the notation registry: buying 10 YES units at USD 0.40 with an illustrative flat fee of USD 0.01 per unit. You should also know that a market midpoint \(m\) is a forecasting benchmark, not something you can actually buy or sell at. This chapter does not reintroduce probability estimation, order-book mechanics, or settlement rules from first principles; it assumes you already have a forecast and a live-looking quote and asks what a disciplined trader does next.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to: state the difference between a modeled probability and the decision threshold implied by executable prices; compute a conservative estimated edge that already accounts for the correct side of the book, fee rounding, and depth, without double-subtracting the spread; explain why abstention is a legitimate output of a decision procedure rather than a bug; compute the cash a buy order must reserve and why a sell order cannot be sized past owned inventory; distinguish a screened "candidate" decision from an actual fill; state the maximum loss of a simple hold-to-settlement position; and compare holding to settlement against selling early through an executable bid.

    17.1 A forecast is not a trade¶

    Suppose your model reports \(\hat p = 0.55\) for some event contract's YES outcome. That number, by itself, tells you nothing about whether to trade. It says nothing about whether an order can currently be filled, at what price, in what quantity, net of what costs, or with what capital already committed elsewhere. A probability estimate is an input to a decision procedure, not the decision itself.

    The forecast-to-order problem is the translation step: given \(\hat p\), an executable quote, a fee schedule, a risk budget, and the trader's existing position, produce one of a small number of disciplined actions. In this book's companion library that translation is implemented as screen_buy_yes in prediction_market_lab.risk, and its output is a Decision record with a status, a reason, a quantity, an executable cost, and a modeled expected profit. The function is deliberately narrow: it screens a single proposed buy-YES order against one book snapshot. It does not place a live order, and its "candidate" status is conditional arithmetic, not evidence of a realized edge.

    Two failure modes motivate this chapter. The first is treating \(\hat p\) as if it were an executable price: comparing a forecast directly to a decision rule such as "buy whenever \(\hat p > 0.5\)" ignores every cost of actually transacting. The second is computing a "cost" using a market midpoint, a last trade, or a candle's low, none of which you could actually have paid. Both failures manufacture an edge that a careful order-level calculation removes.

    17.2 Executable prices, sides, and depth¶

    An event contract's order book has two sides. The ask is what a buyer must pay; the bid is what a seller receives. A buy order sweeps asks starting from the best (lowest) price; a sell order sweeps bids starting from the best (highest) price. Because a book has finite depth at each price level, filling a requested quantity \(Q\) may require paying several different prices, not one.

    The companion's OrderBook.executable(side) returns the asks for a buy and the bids for a sell, and sweep_cost(book, side, qty, fees) walks that side level by level, accumulating both the traded notional and the fee due at each level, and raises an error if the visible depth cannot fill the entire requested quantity. This models an all-or-nothing full-size sweep: a stateless candidate calculation of what the order would cost if it filled completely against the current snapshot, not a live fill.

    Consider an ask side with two levels: 5 units at USD 0.40, then 10 units at USD 0.44. A buy order for 10 units consumes all 5 units at USD 0.40 and 5 of the 10 units at USD 0.44. Running this through the installed library with a flat USD 0.01-per-unit fee gives an executable cost of USD 4.30, not the USD 4.10 you would compute by pricing the entire order at the best level alone. The difference, USD 0.20 here, is exactly the cost of the depth actually consumed at the second, worse level. A decision procedure that only looks at the best bid/ask, ignoring depth, silently overstates the edge on any order large enough to walk the book.

    17.3 Per-unit fees, total fees, and rounding¶

    The canonical fee is an invented USD 0.01 per unit; ten units at USD 0.40 cost USD 4.10. Actual schedules can depend on price, side, category, size, and how fills accumulate. For the companion, define cent ceiling by \(\lceil x\rceil_{0.01}=0.01\lceil x/0.01\rceil\) when \(x\) is a USD amount. FeeSchedule.total(qty, unit_price) applies this cent ceiling to qty * (per_unit + unit_price * notional_rate), once per call. It does not round to whole dollars, as an unqualified mathematical ceiling would. That per-call distinction matters.

    Take a fee schedule with zero flat component and a 0.3% notional rate, applied to a price of USD 0.40. Computing the fee for a single fill of 3 units gives USD 0.01 after rounding up. Now suppose the same 3 units instead fill as three separate 1-unit executions, perhaps because the book only offered one unit at a time. Each 1-unit fill independently rounds its own fee up to USD 0.01, for a total of USD 0.03 across the three fills, three times the fee of the single 3-unit fill. Nothing about the trader's intended size changed; only the number of separate fills did. This is not a hypothetical curiosity: Kalshi's own documentation on fee rounding explicitly distinguishes a trade fee, a separate rounding fee, a rebate, balance precision, and an accumulator applied across fills, which is a different structure from a single flat per-unit number. KALSHI_ROUNDING Polymarket's documentation similarly describes taker fees that are price- and market-dependent rather than a single constant. POLYMARKET_FEES The lesson generalizes beyond either venue: never assume a total cost equals quantity times a single per-unit number without checking whether the venue's actual schedule rounds per fill, depends on price, or depends on market category. A backtest or screening tool that assumes a flat schedule when the real schedule is fill-sensitive will misstate exactly the fills that are hardest to get in one piece: thin, illiquid books.

    17.4 A conservative estimated edge¶

    With executable prices and correctly computed fees in hand, the modeled expected profit of buying \(Q\) YES units at cost \(C(Q)\), holding to ordinary binary settlement, is

    \[ \mathbb{E}_{\hat p}[\Pi] = \hat p \cdot Q - C(Q). \]

    Using the canonical numbers, \(\hat p = 0.55\), \(Q = 10\), and a full ask-side sweep at USD 0.40 per unit with the flat USD 0.01 fee gives \(C(Q) = 4.10\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{\hat p}[\Pi] = 10 \times 0.55 - 4.10 = 1.40\). Running screen_buy_yes(0.55, book, 10, fees, budget, ...) against a sufficiently deep book at that price reproduces exactly this: an executable cost of USD 4.10 and a modeled expected profit of USD 1.40, with status PAPER_CANDIDATE_ONLY.

    Two things about this number deserve emphasis. First, the cost already used the executable ask, so the bid/ask spread has already been paid once; a separate "slippage" deduction using the same spread would double-count it. Second, the subscript on \(\mathbb{E}_{\hat p}\) is not decorative. This is an expectation under a forecast that might be wrong. It is not a promise about the next realized outcome, and it is not, by itself, evidence of a statistically supported edge; Chapter 19 covers what would be required to make that stronger claim. Wolfers and Zitzewitz's working paper describes conditions under which a market price approximates an average belief, and biased approximations when those conditions fail; a model's own \(\hat p\) inherits the same caution in reverse, since a plausible-looking forecast is not automatically well calibrated. WZ2006

    Now walk the thin two-level book from Section 17.2 through the same screen. The executable cost was USD 4.30, so the modeled expected profit becomes \(10 \times 0.55 - 4.30 = 1.20\), USD 0.20 lower purely from depth. Running the actual function on that book confirms this: Decision(status='PAPER_CANDIDATE_ONLY', ..., executable_cost=Decimal('4.30'), modeled_expected_profit=Decimal('1.20')).

    17.5 Abstention is a valid output¶

    A screening procedure needs an explicit rule for when not to trade, and that rule needs a buffer, not merely a strict-inequality comparison to zero. Consider \(\hat p = 0.41\) against the same USD 0.40 ask and USD 0.01 fee, giving a cost of USD 4.10 for 10 units. The modeled expected profit is \(10 \times 0.41 - 4.10 = 0.00\) exactly. Passing this to screen_buy_yes returns NO_TRADE with the reason "modeled edge does not exceed the stated cost buffer" and a computed expected profit of USD 0.00. Nudging the forecast down slightly, to \(\hat p = 0.409\), gives a modeled expected profit of USD \(-0.01\) and the same NO_TRADE status.

    This "does not exceed" behavior is deliberate: a zero or negative modeled edge cannot justify a trade, and the function additionally accepts a minimum_modeled_profit buffer parameter so a caller can require a strictly positive margin over zero before treating an estimate as interesting at all. Requiring \(\mathbb{E}_{\hat p}[\Pi] > 0.50\) against the canonical USD 1.40 estimate still passes; requiring \(\mathbb{E}_{\hat p}[\Pi] > 2.00\) correctly abstains despite the same USD 1.40 figure, because a modeled point estimate this close to a threshold provides little protection against forecast error. Depth can also force abstention directly: attempting to buy 10 units against a book offering only 4 units total raises an "insufficient executable depth for the requested full size" condition inside the sweep, and the screen returns NO_TRADE with exactly that reason, cost and expected profit both left unset. Abstention is not a defect to be silently patched around with a partial fill assumption; it is the correct output when the stated conditions are not met.

    17.6 Quantity, margin, and full collateral¶

    A buy order reserves cash before execution. The companion allows for separate one-unit fills: FeeSchedule.buy_reserve(qty, limit) equals quantity times the sum of the cent-ceiling limit and total(1, limit). Ten units at USD 0.40 with a flat USD 0.01 fee reserve USD 4.10. Under Section 17.3's notional-rate fee, three units at USD 0.40 reserve USD 1.23, although one aggregated fill costs USD 1.21. The reserve is an intentionally conservative bound, not the actual charged notional: fill notional rounds half-up to cents, whereas the reserve rounds each potential one-unit notional upward. Checking only the optimistic sweep cost can therefore underfund fragmented execution.

    A RiskBudget enforces two independent caps: max_order_cost, an upper bound on a single order's cost or reserve, and max_committed_cash, an upper bound on that cost plus whatever cash is already committed to open positions and other resting orders. Both are checked in permits(cost, available_cash, committed_cash). Setting max_order_cost to USD 2.00 against the canonical USD 4.10 order correctly returns NO_TRADE with reason "cash/reservation or loss budget fails," even though the modeled expected profit of USD 1.40 is unchanged; a favorable modeled edge does not override an explicit risk limit. The same rejection occurs if available cash is only USD 3.00, or if USD 2.00 is already committed against a USD 5.00 cap.

    Selling has a mirrored but asymmetric constraint: there is no borrowing or shorting in this classroom ledger. A sell order's quantity is checked against owned inventory minus units already reserved by other resting sell orders on the same contract; attempting to sell units you do not hold, or double-reserving units already committed to an unfilled sell order, is explicitly rejected as a "naked or doubly reserved sell." Margin for a purchase is a cash question; margin for a sale is an inventory question, and conflating the two produces either an impossible short or a double-counted position.

    17.7 Conditional fills, not guaranteed fills¶

    A PAPER_CANDIDATE_ONLY decision describes conditional arithmetic: if the order is actually submitted and if it actually fills at the assumed prices, this is the resulting cost and modeled profit. Neither condition is guaranteed by the screen itself. Submitting an order creates a resting or partially resting obligation; only a subsequent fill event, driven by an actual counterparty or an explicit taker sweep against a real book snapshot, converts that candidate into cash flow and inventory.

    This distinction matters most for resting limit orders placed below the current ask (to buy) or above the current bid (to sell), waiting for the market to move to them. Observing that a later quote merely touched your limit price is not evidence of a fill; queue position, size ahead of you, and the exact sequence of trades at that price are usually unobservable from public data. The companion's paper broker explicitly encodes this: calling its resting-quote observation method on an order whose limit price has been touched returns the literal message "NO_INFERRED_FILL: quotes alone do not establish queue position or a trade," and it does not credit cash or inventory. A fill-conditioned forecast, the forecast appropriate at the moment a resting order finally fills, can also differ from the forecast held when the order was placed; a counterparty willing to trade at your resting price may be reacting to exactly the information that makes the fill less favorable to you, an instance of adverse selection covered further in Chapter 21.

    17.8 Maximum loss¶

    For the simple hold-to-settlement position in this chapter, the maximum loss is bounded and known in advance: it is the cost already paid, because the worst outcome for a bought YES position is an ordinary NO settlement paying zero per unit. Running an actual settlement of payout zero against the canonical position, 10 units bought at USD 0.40 with a USD 0.01-per-unit fee for a total cost of USD 4.10, realizes exactly USD \(-4.10\): cash is unchanged from its post-purchase level, and realized profit is precisely the negative of the amount paid. This is a useful sanity check for any scenario table: the NO-settlement column of a payout matrix should reproduce the negative of the initial cost, no less and no more, when there is no exit fee on settlement itself. An exceptional non-YES/NO payout, such as a half-dollar settlement, produces an intermediate outcome; the same worked position settled at a USD 0.50 payout realizes USD 0.90, not the full USD 5.90 of an ordinary YES nor the full USD \(-4.10\) of an ordinary NO. Kalshi's settlement documentation explicitly distinguishes ordinary YES/NO settlement from a scalar or sub-cent settlement path with its own fee and rounding treatment, and states that settlement timing itself can vary, which is a separate fact from the size of the payout. KALSHI_SETTLEMENT Do not assume every contract shares the ordinary binary maximum-loss shape; check the actual settlement rule before relying on "I can only lose what I paid."

    17.9 Holding to settlement versus selling early¶

    Holding to settlement realizes exactly the contractual payout net of the original cost, with no further transaction. Selling early instead realizes the executable bid at the time of exit, net of whatever exit fee applies, and locks in that value regardless of the eventual settlement. Using the notation-registry convention, selling the canonical 10-unit position at a bid of USD 0.46 with a further USD 0.01-per-unit exit fee realizes \(10 \times (0.46 - 0.40 - 0.01 - 0.01) = 0.40\). Running the broker's own sell-and-fill path against that bid, with the same fee schedule applied on the way out, reproduces this exactly: realized profit of USD 0.40 after the sale, versus a conservative liquidation mark (not yet an actual sale) of the same USD 0.40 computed from the identical bid and fee before any sell order is even submitted. The mark and the realized outcome agree here because the mark uses the same fee-inclusive bid arithmetic; they can diverge once the mark instead relies on a stale, thin, or since-moved book.

    The decision between holding and selling is a genuine trade-off, not a dominance relationship. Holding retains full exposure to the contractual payout, whatever it eventually is, and locks up capital until settlement with no early exit fee in the simplest classroom case. Selling caps the outcome at a currently known, worse-than-full-payout number, releases capital sooner, and pays an exit fee that holding to ordinary settlement does not. Neither choice is uniformly better; it depends on updated beliefs about the outcome, the capital's next best use, and the marginal cost of the exit itself.

    Exercises¶

    Exercise 17.1. A book's ask side has 3 units at USD 0.52 and 8 units at USD 0.55. A trader wants to buy 6 units, with a flat fee of USD 0.02 per unit. Compute the executable cost, using the correct price at each level, and the modeled expected profit at \(\hat p = 0.60\).

    Solution. The first 3 units cost \(3 \times 0.52 = 1.56\); the remaining 3 units come from the second level at \(3 \times 0.55 = 1.65\). Notional is \(1.56 + 1.65 = 3.21\). The fee is \(6 \times 0.02 = 0.12\), so total cost is USD 3.33. Modeled expected profit is \(6 \times 0.60 - 3.33 = 3.60 - 3.33 = 0.27\).

    Exercise 17.2. Using the same book and fee as Exercise 17.1, at what integer modeled probability threshold (to the nearest whole percentage point) does the modeled expected profit first become nonpositive, holding quantity fixed at 6 units? State the exact breakeven \(\hat p\).

    Solution. Breakeven requires \(6\hat p = 3.33\), so \(\hat p = 0.555\). At \(\hat p = 0.55\) the modeled expected profit is \(3.30 - 3.33 = -0.03\), negative; at \(\hat p = 0.56\) it is \(3.36 - 3.33 = 0.03\), positive. The exact breakeven is 55.5 percent; any screen using a strict "greater than zero" rule would trade at 56 percent and abstain at 55 percent, with no trade at exactly the breakeven value itself.

    Exercise 17.3. A trader holds 10 YES units bought for USD 4.10 total. Write the ordinary binary profit values, then show that a half-dollar-per-unit settlement gives their arithmetic average under these fixed-cost assumptions. Explain why this does not assign 50 percent probability to any outcome.

    Solution. Ordinary YES settlement realizes \(10 \times 1 - 4.10 = 5.90\); ordinary NO settlement realizes \(10 \times 0 - 4.10 = -4.10\). Their arithmetic average is USD 0.90, which happens to match a half-dollar exceptional settlement's realized profit of \(10 \times 0.5 - 4.10 = 0.90\) only because this contract's payout function is linear in the settlement value and the cost is fixed; it is not a general property, and it does not imply the exceptional payout has 50 percent probability or that it always falls exactly between the ordinary outcomes for a differently structured contract.

    Exercise 17.4. Explain, without computing new numbers, why a risk budget check that only compares an order's optimistic sweep cost to max_order_cost, and ignores the conservative buy-reserve amount, could later permit an order that the budget was actually meant to block.

    Solution. The sweep cost assumes the order fills exactly as one calculation against the current snapshot at the displayed level prices. The buy-reserve amount instead assumes the worst case of separate one-unit fills, each rounding its own fee up. When the fee schedule has a nonzero notional-rate component, the reserve can exceed the sweep cost, as shown for the 3-unit notional-rate example in Section 17.6. A budget check against the smaller sweep-cost figure alone could approve an order whose actual worst-case cash commitment exceeds the intended cap once fills fragment.

    Exercise 17.5. A position was bought at a total cost of USD 4.10. State, in one sentence each, the difference between its "marked" profit from a conservative liquidation calculation and its "realized" profit, and explain which one changes if the reference bid used for the mark moves before an actual sell order is filled.

    Solution. Marked profit is a valuation using a specified current bid and fee, without any executed sale; realized profit requires an actual (or explicitly simulated) sell fill and is fixed once that fill occurs. If the reference bid moves before a sell order actually fills, the mark changes to reflect the new bid, but realized profit remains undefined until a fill occurs and is then determined by the price actually achieved, not by any earlier mark.

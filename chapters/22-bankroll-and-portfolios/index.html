    22. Bankroll, portfolios, and survival¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Read from beliefs to decisions, the forecast-to-order decision, and Chapter 21 before this chapter. You should be comfortable with expected value under a stated model, the canonical accounting example in the notation registry, and the glossary entries for bankroll, collateral, capital lockup, drawdown, and Kelly criterion.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to derive a constant-cost binary log-wealth sizing fraction, distinguish cash spent from payout notional, explain the limits of fractional Kelly, and build joint scenario stress tests. You should also distinguish an outstanding-commitment cap from a drawdown limit and recognize when depth or fee rounding invalidates a constant-unit-cost sizing formula.

    22.1 Why expected value alone is not a sizing rule¶

    Chapters 3 and 17 computed positive modeled expected profit at \(\hat p=0.55\) and USD 0.41 all-in cost. That does not determine size. For independent repeated binary bets with constant success probability \(0<p<1\), betting the entire bankroll survives \(n\) rounds with probability \(p^n\), which tends to zero. One loss ends the sequence even when each bet has positive expected value.

    Expected log wealth is one risk-sensitive sizing objective, not the only possible objective. Kelly's inspected example contrasts log growth with expected capital; his conclusion limits its comparison to fixed allocations per received symbol and requires reinvestment and stake control. His track-take treatment permits leaving capital unbet. The source's independent-symbol communication model is not an estimated event-market forecast. The derivation below is independent classroom algebra; changing Kelly's base-two log to natural log rescales growth without changing its maximizing fraction. KELLY1956

    22.2 Deriving the sizing fraction for a fully collateralized binary bet¶

    Consider a bankroll \(W\) held as available cash, with no other positions. Spend a fraction \(\phi\in[0,1]\) buying YES at constant all-in unit cost \(c\in(0,1)\), normalized to a USD 1 payout. We use \(\phi\), not the notation registry's fee symbol \(f\), for the bankroll fraction. Treating units as divisible gives quantity \(\phi W/c\). In this hold-to-settlement calculation, the entire \(\phi W\) is paid up front, there is no borrowing, and an ordinary NO pays nothing. Early resale is a different decision.

    If the contract settles YES, the units bought pay USD 1 each, so terminal wealth is the untouched remainder plus the payout:

    \[ W_{\text{YES}} = W(1-\phi) + \frac{\phi W}{c}\cdot 1 = W\left[1 + \phi\,\frac{1-c}{c}\right]. \]

    If it settles NO, the spent fraction is lost entirely:

    \[ W_{\text{NO}} = W(1-\phi). \]

    Expected log growth per bet, as a function of the spent fraction \(\phi\), is

    \[ G(\phi) = p\,\ln\!\left(1+\phi\,\frac{1-c}{c}\right) + (1-p)\,\ln(1-\phi), \]

    where \(p\) is the trader's model probability of YES. Differentiating and setting the derivative to zero (writing \(k=(1-c)/c\) for brevity):

    \[ \frac{dG}{d\phi} = \frac{pk}{1+\phi k} - \frac{1-p}{1-\phi} = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; pk(1-\phi) = (1-p)(1+\phi k). \]

    Expanding and collecting terms in \(\phi\):

    \[ pk - pk\phi = (1-p) + (1-p)k\phi \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; pk-(1-p) = \phi k\big[(1-p)+p\big] = \phi k. \]

    So \(\phi=p-(1-p)/k=p-(1-p)c/(1-c)\), giving the interior solution:

    \[ \phi^{*} = \frac{p-c}{1-c}. \]

    For \(0<p<1\) and \(\phi<1\), the second derivative is \(-pk^2/(1+\phi k)^2-(1-p)/(1-\phi)^2<0\), proving strict concavity and that the feasible stationary point is a maximum. Spending all cash gives minus-infinite expected log growth when NO has positive probability. At \(p=0.55,c=0.41\), \(\phi^*=0.14/0.59\approx0.2373\): about 23.7 percent of bankroll. At \(p=0.39\), the unconstrained fraction is negative, so the long-only optimum is zero. Including the boundary beliefs gives \(\phi^*=\max(0,\min(1,(p-c)/(1-c)))\), with the endpoint limits interpreted under the stated belief. This is conditional optimization, not permission to treat an estimated probability as certain.

    22.3 Two fractions that happen to coincide here¶

    The fraction of bankroll spent, \(\phi\), equals the fraction lost on an ordinary NO under these assumptions. It is not the same as face-value payout exposure: \(\phi W/c\) units can have a USD 1 payout notional larger than the amount spent when \(c<1\). Full funding removes borrowing from this classroom position, not its potentially large percentage price sensitivity. Keep cash spent, maximum loss, and payout notional separate. A statement such as "I risk 5 percent" is meaningful only after specifying that the whole spent 5 percent can be lost; calling it small does not establish affordability.

    22.4 Fractional Kelly under parameter uncertainty¶

    The single-bet derivation assumes a specified \(p\); interpreting repeated use as a long-run growth result needs additional repeated-opportunity and reinvestment assumptions. In practice \(p\) has estimation and specification error, and opportunities can be dependent and nonstationary. Locally around the interior maximum, a small reduction in \(\phi\) has a second-order effect on modeled expected growth, while reducing cash lost in a NO outcome. This motivates fractional Kelly: use \(\kappa\phi^*\) for a chosen \(\kappa\in(0,1]\). It does not derive a universally safe \(\kappa\) or guarantee a drawdown limit.

    At \(\kappa = 0.25\) (quarter Kelly) with \(p=0.55\), \(c=0.41\): spend \(0.25 \times 0.2373 \approx 0.0593\), or about 5.93 percent of bankroll. This number is not arbitrary caution for its own sake; it is a deliberate trade of some expected growth for materially lower variance under the acknowledged possibility that the modeled 0.55 is wrong. A useful way to see the cost of two consecutive losing outcomes at this fraction: wealth after two independent NO settlements shrinks to \((1-0.0593)^2 \approx 0.8849\) of its starting value — an 11.5 percent drawdown from just two losing bets, even at a sizing fraction chosen specifically to be conservative. Full Kelly at the same \(p,c\) would produce a much larger two-loss drawdown of \((1-0.2373)^2 \approx 0.582\), a 41.8 percent decline. Aggressive sizing amplifies the ordinary cost of being wrong, not just the reward for being right.

    22.5 Correlated positions and joint scenario matrices¶

    A trader rarely holds an isolated position. Two contracts can share an election, release, or disputed source, making independence implausible. Correlation is one summary of dependence, not an assumption of a particular distribution shape. For a transparent loss check, enumerate plausible joint scenarios and compute portfolio payoff in each without first estimating their probabilities. If probabilities are later attached, they must form a coherent joint model rather than independent marginals chosen for convenience.

    Consider two positions, each 10 units bought at an all-in cost of USD 0.41 per unit (total cost USD 4.10 apiece, USD 8.20 combined), on two contracts labeled event-a and event-b. Three joint scenarios: both settle NO (perhaps a shared adverse driver), both settle YES, or they diverge (one YES, one NO). Under the shared-bad-news scenario, receipts are zero on both, so portfolio profit is \(0 - 8.20 = -\text{USD }8.20\): the entire combined cost basis is lost at once, even though a naive independence assumption might have priced the chance of both failing as small. Under both-YES, receipts are \(10+10=20\), so profit is \(20-8.20=\text{USD }11.80\). Under divergence, receipts are 10, so profit is \(10-8.20=\text{USD }1.80\). Writing down these three numbers directly, rather than multiplying two marginal probabilities as if the positions were independent, is what actually protects a sizing decision from an unstated dependence assumption: the worst case (minus USD 8.20) is visible in the matrix regardless of how the two events are correlated, because it does not depend on estimating that correlation at all.

    22.6 A cash risk budget as a hard ceiling on committed collateral¶

    The isolated sizing fraction does not prevent excessive aggregate commitments. A cash budget therefore caps both single-order cost and total open cost basis plus reservations. Start with USD 10.00 cash, no commitments, and USD 5.00 caps on each. A USD 4.10 position passes, leaving USD 5.90 cash and USD 4.10 open cost basis. A second USD 4.10 candidate fits the remaining cash but would bring commitments to USD 8.20, exceeding the cap. The companion's risk.py illustrates that second-position check. Per-order affordability and portfolio commitment are separate requirements; the existence of sufficient cash alone does not satisfy both.

    22.7 Affordability, drawdown, and capital lockup together¶

    Three constraints interact and are easy to conflate. Affordability asks whether the trader can absorb the maximum possible loss on a position or portfolio without disrupting other obligations; it is a precondition, not a growth-rate optimization, and no Kelly fraction or confidence level removes the requirement to check it directly. Drawdown is the realized decline from a prior peak in bankroll; Section 22.4 already showed that even a deliberately conservative fractional-Kelly size produces a double-digit-percentage drawdown from just two losing outcomes, so a sizing rule's "on average" property is compatible with periods of substantial, real decline. Capital lockup is the duration for which spent cash is unavailable for anything else, from order submission until settlement (which official documentation shows can take a variable amount of time depending on the specific market and clearing process, not a single fixed interval). KALSHI_SETTLEMENT A trader who reports an attractive annualized return by dividing a single trade's percentage profit by a short assumed holding period, while the actual funds were locked for a much longer resolution window, has silently swapped capital lockup for an optimistic assumption; the same nominal edge looks very different once the true lockup period is used as the denominator of any per-period return calculation.

    22.8 Constant fees versus real, price-dependent fee schedules¶

    The closed form assumes that total cost is \(C(Q)=cQ\) throughout the quantities under consideration. A notional-rate fee at one fixed executable price can still satisfy this assumption if rounding is absent. Depth, fixed charges, and fill-dependent rounding can instead make cost nonlinear or discontinuous in quantity. Actual venue fee and accumulator documentation motivates checking that structure, not simply inserting a current average fee into a timeless formula. POLYMARKET_FEES KALSHI_ROUNDING

    If cost depends on size, recomputing \(c=C(Q)/Q\) and plugging it into the constant-cost optimum is generally not an optimization solution. Increasing \(Q\) changes the cost of the next units, so the marginal tradeoff differs from the average cost. With no other positions, the correct quantity objective under the same binary model is

    \[ G_Q(Q)=p\log\frac{W-C(Q)+Q}{W} +(1-p)\log\frac{W-C(Q)}{W}. \]

    For whole-unit teaching orders, compare this objective over feasible quantities, including zero, using the actual sweep cost at each size and the conservative reservation and risk-budget limits. Keep positive wealth in every state with positive model probability. This handles stepped depth and rounding without pretending a derivative exists at every quantity. The companion's fractional_kelly deliberately implements only the constant-cost divisible-unit formula; this generalized optimization is a derivation, not a shipped sizing engine.

    Separately, collateral itself is not always plain cash. Some venues describe a wrapped token representing an underlying stable-asset claim, with its own wrapping, unwrapping, and pause mechanics documented by the operator. POLYMARKET_COLLATERAL A sizing calculation denominated in "dollars of bankroll" implicitly assumes that collateral converts to and from cash without friction or delay; that assumption should be checked against the actual collateral instrument in use, not treated as automatically true.

    22.9 No risk-free leverage¶

    This classroom ledger has no borrowing: cash and reservations bound feasible purchases. At constant unit cost, spending fraction \(\phi\) buys \(\phi W/c\) units. Those units' face payout can exceed the cash spent, but no lender finances the position and no loan is implied by that ratio. Full funding is not risk-free exposure, and it is not a claim that every real venue or trader has the same financing arrangement. Neither borrowing nor its absence makes a positive expected-value calculation a guarantee.

    22.10 Assumptions and failure modes¶

    The helper assumes a specified \(p\), binary USD 1 payout, constant unit cost in \((0,1)\), divisible units, no extra exit or settlement fees, and no other holdings. Existing positions require joint terminal-wealth analysis; adding isolated Kelly fractions is not a general portfolio solution, even for independent simultaneous bets. A scenario table exposes possible losses without estimating their probabilities, but optimizing expected log wealth across scenarios additionally needs a joint probability model. Whole-unit grids and nonlinear costs require feasible-quantity checks rather than mechanically rounding \(\phi W/c\) upward. None of this chapter's arithmetic establishes that an estimated \(p\) is correct.

    22.11 Companion example¶

    The companion script risk.py implements exactly the fractional-sizing formula and the joint-scenario matrix derived above, plus the cash-budget check. Running it reports a quarter-Kelly fraction of cash spent of 0.059322 at the stated \(p=0.55\), \(c=0.41\) assumptions (matching Section 22.4), a zero fraction when the belief is below cost, joint scenario profits of minus USD 8.20 (shared bad news), USD 11.80 (both YES), and USD 1.80 (divergent) exactly as derived in Section 22.5, and a rejected second position against a USD 5.00 total commitment cap exactly as derived in Section 22.6. It also rejects an invalid binary cost outside \((0,1)\), since the closed form is undefined there. Run it with:

    Command working directory: run this chapter's Python commands from book\companion. From the trading repository root, run Set-Location book\companion once first. The .venv belongs to that companion project; see companion setup.

    .\.venv\Scripts\python.exe examples\risk.py

    Every number above is deterministic arithmetic under explicitly stated assumptions; none of it is an empirical measurement of a real trading account's growth or drawdown.

    Exercises and solution sketches¶

    Exercise 1: the sizing fraction at a different edge¶

    Compute \(\phi^{*}\) for \(p=0.60\) and \(c=0.45\), then the quarter-Kelly spend fraction.

    Solution sketch. \(\phi^{*} = (0.60-0.45)/(1-0.45) = 0.15/0.55 \approx 0.2727\). Quarter Kelly: \(0.25 \times 0.2727 \approx 0.0682\), about 6.8 percent of bankroll.

    Exercise 2: two-loss drawdown at full Kelly versus an eighth Kelly¶

    Using \(p=0.55, c=0.41\) (full Kelly \(\phi^{*}\approx0.2373\)), compute the wealth remaining after two consecutive NO outcomes at full Kelly and at an eighth-Kelly fraction (\(\kappa=0.125\)), and comment on the trade-off.

    Solution sketch. Full Kelly: \((1-0.2373)^2 \approx 0.582\), a 41.8 percent drawdown. Eighth Kelly: spend fraction \(0.125\times0.2373\approx0.0297\); \((1-0.0297)^2\approx0.9414\), a 5.9 percent drawdown. The smaller fraction trades away growth rate for a much smaller realized decline from the same two adverse outcomes.

    Exercise 3: a third correlated scenario¶

    Expand Section 22.5's combined "divergent" row into the specific state "event-a YES, event-b NO," and compute portfolio profit.

    Solution sketch. Receipts: \(10(1) + 10(0) = 10\). Profit: \(10 - 8.20 = \text{USD }1.80\), identical to the "divergent" scenario already listed, since the payoff only depends on which single leg wins, not which specific leg it is, given identical costs and quantities on each side.

    Exercise 4: when the constant-cost formula stops applying¶

    A fee is 2 percent of notional plus a flat per-unit component. Explain when the constant-\(c\) formula still applies and why depth or fill-dependent rounding can require a different quantity optimization.

    Solution sketch. At a fixed executable price with no rounding or other size effects, this fee remains linear in quantity and gives a constant all-in \(c\). If larger quantities sweep worse prices or split into differently rounded fills, \(C(Q)\) is no longer \(cQ\). Evaluate Section 22.8's quantity-level log-wealth objective under actual cost and reservation limits; substituting \(C(Q)/Q\) into the constant-cost optimum ignores the changed marginal cost and need not maximize growth.

    Exercise 5: leverage intuition that does not transfer¶

    A trader argues, "I'm only risking 10 percent of my account on this position, so it's low-risk even if I'm wrong." Using Section 22.3, identify the hidden assumption in this statement as applied to a fully collateralized binary contract.

    Solution sketch. It assumes that calling a possible loss "10 percent" makes it affordable or unlikely. Here all of the spent 10 percent can be lost on NO; the position's payout notional can be larger than that cash amount without borrowing. Check the full loss against obligations and other commitments, rather than inferring safety from the percentage or from full funding alone.

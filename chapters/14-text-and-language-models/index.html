    14. Text, news, and language models¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Read Chapter 11 for the logistic-regression and likelihood foundations reused here, and Chapter 13 for the point-in-time discipline applied to evolving information. You should be comfortable with basic probability and simple algebra (logarithms, sums, ratios). The notation registry and the book's boundary between synthetic and real evidence, described in the research README, both apply directly to this chapter's discussion of language models trained on enormous, largely unverifiable historical corpora.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to: convert text into numeric features using bag-of-words and TF-IDF, and derive the inverse-document-frequency weighting from first principles; explain why a vocabulary or document-frequency statistic must be estimated only from training-period text to avoid leakage; describe, at a conceptual level, how dense embeddings and retrieval differ from sparse bag-of-words representations, and how a pretrained large language model's frozen weights interact with a decision cutoff; and state, precisely, why a language model's self-reported confidence is not automatically a calibrated probability and why training-corpus contamination is a distinct, serious threat to any retrospective evaluation of text-based forecasting.

    14.1 Text as evidence, and why to start simple¶

    News bulletins, official statements, and market commentary are an obviously rich source of information about many prediction-market questions, and it is tempting to reach immediately for the most capable available language model. This chapter argues for the opposite discipline: build the simplest defensible text-to-probability pipeline first, understand exactly what it does and does not use, and only then discuss richer representations, with their much larger and much harder to audit set of assumptions.

    The companion script for this chapter and the next, text_and_neural.py, fits a bag-of-words TF-IDF logistic model (this chapter) and a small neural network on the same numeric features used in Chapters 11–12 (Chapter 15). Run it from the companion project root (book\companion):

    Command working directory: run this chapter's Python commands from book\companion. From the trading repository root, run Set-Location book\companion once first. The .venv belongs to that companion project; see companion setup.

    .\.venv\Scripts\python.exe examples\text_and_neural.py

    Its text is entirely invented: a short templated sentence per synthetic event, such as "synthetic bulletin reports improving conditions with 12 hours remaining," generated from the same toy process that produces the numeric features elsewhere in this book. It is a mechanism demonstration, not a test of news understanding, and the script's own reported conclusion says so directly.

    14.2 From words to numbers: bag-of-words and TF-IDF¶

    The simplest text representation, bag-of-words, discards word order entirely and retains only which terms occur, and how often, in a document. MANNING2008 documents this representation and its refinements; the discarding of order is a real limitation ("supply concerns fall" and "concerns supply fall" become the same bag) accepted in exchange for a much simpler, much more tractable representation, especially at small sample sizes.

    Raw term counts overweight common words shared across almost every document ("report", "the", "today") relative to words that actually distinguish one document from another. Term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) corrects for this. For a term \(t\) in document \(d\), term frequency is typically a normalized count, \(\mathrm{tf}(t,d) = \text{count of }t\text{ in }d / \text{length of }d\). Inverse document frequency downweights terms that occur in many documents:

    \[ \mathrm{idf}(t) = \log\!\frac{N}{\mathrm{df}(t)}, \]

    where \(N\) is the number of documents in the reference corpus and \(\mathrm{df}(t)\) is the number of documents containing \(t\) at all (not its total count). MANNING2008 specifies exactly this document-frequency-based construction, distinguishing it from a raw-frequency-based weighting. The TF-IDF weight of a term in a document is the product \(\mathrm{tf}(t,d)\times\mathrm{idf}(t)\).

    Work a tiny, fully worked corpus. Three four-word documents: "improving demand growth report", "weak demand report today", "improving supply report today". With \(N=3\): the term "report" appears in all three documents, so \(\mathrm{df}(\text{report})=3\) and \(\mathrm{idf}(\text{report})=\log(3/3)=0\) — a term occurring in every document receives zero weight regardless of its raw frequency, exactly the intended effect. The term "growth" appears in only the first document, so \(\mathrm{df}(\text{growth})=1\) and \(\mathrm{idf}(\text{growth})=\log(3/1)=1.098612\); with term frequency \(1/4=0.25\) in that document, its TF-IDF weight is \(0.274653\). The term "demand" appears in two of the three documents, giving \(\mathrm{idf}(\text{demand})=\log(3/2)=0.405465\) and, at the same term frequency, a TF-IDF weight of \(0.101366\) (all values checked with a script). The pattern is exactly as intended: a word appearing in every document (uninformative for distinguishing them) is weighted to zero, while a word appearing in only one document is weighted most heavily.

    The resulting TF-IDF vector for each document — one number per vocabulary term — is then fed into an ordinary logistic regression, exactly as in Chapter 11, except that \(x\) is now a (typically sparse) TF-IDF vector rather than a small set of hand-defined numeric features. Nothing about the fitting or evaluation machinery from Chapters 11–12 changes; only the feature construction does.

    14.3 Reading the companion's TF-IDF pipeline honestly¶

    The companion fits TfidfVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), min_df=2) followed by logistic regression, using training rows only. Unigrams and bigrams retain individual words and adjacent two-word phrases, discarding terms in fewer than two training documents. Its defaults differ from Section 14.2's unsmoothed hand example: it multiplies raw term counts by \(\log((1+N)/(1+\mathrm{df}(t)))+1\), then normalizes each document vector to unit Euclidean length. A ubiquitous term therefore has IDF one, not zero, before normalization. These are explicitly different weighting conventions, not conflicting arithmetic; the chapter's hand TF-IDF numbers are not the vectorizer's literal outputs.

    The vocabulary and document frequencies are themselves fitted quantities. Estimating them on test-period text would change which terms the model can use and their weights based on information outside the training boundary, just as fitting a numeric scaler on test rows would.

    Running the companion script reports a training vocabulary of 21 terms — small, because the underlying bulletins are drawn from a narrow invented template, not because 21 is a target to aim for on real text. On the fixture's 45 test events, the TF-IDF logistic model scores Brier 0.206632 and log loss 0.602748, close to (and here very slightly worse than) Chapter 11's numeric-feature logistic model's Brier 0.198372. This is not evidence that TF-IDF text features are weaker than the numeric signal feature in general; the invented bulletin text was constructed as a nearly deterministic function of the same underlying signal used to build the numeric feature, so the two representations carry closely related information in this particular fixture, and neither comparison should be read as a general statement about news text versus structured numeric signals.

    14.4 Beyond bag-of-words: dense embeddings and retrieval¶

    Bag-of-words and TF-IDF represent each term independently: "growth" and "expansion" share no representation at all, even though they are related in meaning, because the vocabulary is just a list of distinct strings. Dense embeddings address this by representing each term (or, more commonly today, each token in context) as a vector of continuous numbers, learned so that semantically related terms end up with similar vectors. A document or query can then be represented by combining its token embeddings, and similarity between two pieces of text can be measured as a distance between vectors rather than an overlap of exact vocabulary terms.

    Retrieval is the complementary idea of using such a representation to find the most relevant passages from a larger corpus — prior bulletins, official rule documents, historical commentary — given a query, before those passages are used as features or fed to a downstream model. This can be valuable specifically because a prediction-market question often depends on a small number of highly relevant documents buried in a much larger stream, and a well-built retrieval step can surface those documents more effectively than a fixed bag-of-words match on exact wording.

    Neither dense embeddings nor a retrieval pipeline is implemented in this book's companion; text_and_neural.py's text branch is the bag-of-words/TF-IDF pipeline described in Sections 14.2–14.3, and nothing more. This section describes the concept, and names it as a plausible extension a reader might build, without claiming this book has implemented, tuned, or evaluated it.

    14.5 Large language models as forecasters: what is actually being tested¶

    A further, increasingly common idea is to prompt a large pretrained language model directly with a description of an event and ask it to state a probability, or to use it to summarize, extract, or reason over retrieved text before a separate scoring step. DEVLIN2019 documents one influential family of pretrained bidirectional text models, trained on a large text corpus and then adapted with a task-specific output layer through fine-tuning; its abstract is language-benchmark evidence, not evidence about market-forecast quality, and it does not certify any particular model's historical training cutoff.

    That last point is a distinct threat from ordinary overfitting. A model pretrained on an event's reported outcome may retrieve that answer rather than forecast it. Even a training cutoff before resolution can be too late: news arriving after the stated decision time but before resolution is also future information for that forecast. Audit pretraining, fine-tuning, retrieval, and other updates against the decision's information boundary. A claim of historically deployable performance additionally needs an as-of available model and procedure, not merely a modern model with an asserted older corpus cutoff. If training provenance is undisclosed or changing, the study cannot rule out contamination; apparent ignorance in a few prompts is no substitute for that audit.

    A second, related discipline concerns reproducibility. Language model providers routinely update model weights and default behavior behind a stable-looking name or API endpoint. An evaluation that reports results from "the current version of a hosted model" cannot be rerun later and trusted to reproduce the same behavior unless the specific model version and the exact prompt text are both frozen and recorded. Frozen prompts and a frozen, specifically versioned model are a minimum requirement for any language-model-based experiment reported in this space, exactly as this book insists on frozen fitting procedures and fixed random seeds elsewhere.

    14.6 Hallucination and the trap of a self-reported probability¶

    Large language models can produce fluent, plausible-sounding text that is nonetheless factually wrong — a widely observed failure commonly called hallucination: a fabricated citation, an invented statistic, or a confidently stated but incorrect claim about a rule or an event. Any feature, summary, or claim extracted from a language model's output needs independent verification against a primary source before it is trusted as evidence, in exactly the way this book's own citation ledger distinguishes an inspected primary source from an unverified secondary claim. Using an unverified model-generated summary as a modeling feature imports whatever hallucination risk exists in that summary directly into your pipeline.

    A specific instance of this general risk deserves its own warning, because it is easy to be seduced by it: a language model's self-reported numeric confidence ("I estimate a 70% probability") is not automatically a calibrated forecast probability in the sense this book has defined throughout. A proper scoring rule (Chapter 10) makes stating your honest belief the optimal strategy under a specific, stated incentive structure; a language model generating text has no such incentive built in unless it was specifically elicited and evaluated that way, and its stated number is, structurally, just more generated text, subject to the same fluency-without-accuracy risk as any other output. Treating a model's self-reported number as if it had already been checked for calibration skips the entire evaluation discipline this book has built: any such number must be scored, on genuinely held-out, event-grouped, availability-respecting data, exactly like any other forecast in this book, before it earns any trust at all. A confident-sounding number is not evidence of a well-calibrated one.

    14.7 What this book's companion actually offers, and what it does not¶

    To be concrete about scope: this book's default companion examples are entirely offline, deterministic, and use only small, local, CPU-friendly models on invented text; none of them requires a paid API call, a GPU, or a network connection, and none of them implements retrieval, embeddings, or a large pretrained language model. Sections 14.4–14.6 describe genuine, active areas of practice that a reader may reasonably want to build toward, with their own additional risks (contamination, reproducibility, hallucination) layered on top of everything already established in Chapters 7–13 about point-in-time discipline, dependence grouping, and honest evaluation. Building any of that is a substantial additional engineering and evaluation project, not a drop-in replacement for the TF-IDF pipeline demonstrated here, and none of its promise should be assumed without the same held-out, availability-respecting evaluation this book has insisted on throughout.

    14.8 Failure modes¶

    Fitting a vectorizer's vocabulary, document frequencies, or any other text-derived statistic on validation or test documents leaks exactly as surely as fitting a numeric scaler on those rows would. Assuming a pretrained language model's training cutoff based on its release date, rather than documented evidence, risks silently including outcome-relevant information the model may already have encountered during pretraining. Treating a hosted model's current behavior as reproducible without freezing both the exact prompt and a specific, versioned model invites results that cannot be checked or repeated later. Using an unverified language-model-generated claim or summary as a modeling feature imports unverified hallucination risk directly into your pipeline. And trusting a language model's self-reported confidence as if it were already a properly scored, calibrated forecast skips the entire evaluation discipline this book exists to teach.

    Exercises and solution sketches¶

    Exercise 1: computing IDF and TF-IDF by hand¶

    Using the three documents "improving demand growth report", "weak demand report today", "improving supply report today", compute \(\mathrm{idf}\) for "report" (in all three documents), "supply" (in one document), and "today" (in two documents). Then compute the TF-IDF weight of "today" in the second document.

    Solution sketch. \(\mathrm{idf}(\text{report})=\log(3/3)=0\). \(\mathrm{idf}(\text{supply})=\log(3/1)=1.098612\). \(\mathrm{idf}(\text{today})=\log(3/2)=0.405465\). In the second document ("weak demand report today", 4 words), \(\mathrm{tf}(\text{today})=1/4=0.25\), so its TF-IDF weight is \(0.25\times0.405465=0.101366\).

    Exercise 2: why fit the vectorizer on training text only¶

    A colleague proposes fitting the TF-IDF vectorizer's vocabulary on the entire dataset (train, validation, and test text combined) "to make sure no words are missed," then splitting into cohorts afterward for model fitting. Explain the leakage risk.

    Solution sketch. The vocabulary and document-frequency statistics would then reflect terms and their prevalence in validation and test documents, information that would not genuinely be available at the training decision cutoff in a real deployment. A term appearing only in test-period documents, or a document-frequency count shifted by test-period prevalence, could subtly influence which terms receive nonzero weight, contaminating the evaluation exactly as fitting a numeric scaler on test rows would.

    Exercise 3: pretraining contamination¶

    A researcher retrospectively evaluates a pretrained language model's stated probability for an event that resolved two years before the model's documented pretraining cutoff. What specific concern applies here that would not apply to a purely numeric logistic regression fit only on pre-event features?

    Solution sketch. The language model may have encountered text discussing the event's actual outcome during pretraining, since its training corpus predates neither the event nor its resolution. This risks the model's stated "forecast" reflecting memorized post-resolution information rather than genuine ex ante reasoning, a leakage channel specific to models pretrained on broad historical text and not present in a numeric model whose features are explicitly constructed to respect a decision cutoff.

    Exercise 4: is a stated confidence a calibrated forecast?¶

    A language model, prompted with a event description, replies: "I would estimate roughly an 80% chance of this occurring." Under what condition would this number be admissible as a forecast probability in this book's evaluation framework, and what condition would make it inadmissible?

    Solution sketch. It is admissible for evaluation if it is a recorded probability from a frozen, versioned procedure whose information respects the stated decision cutoff, with the eventual contractual label scored afterward. It need not already have a successful evaluation to be tested. It is not yet established as calibrated or useful. A model, retrieval source, or prompt using information after the stated decision time makes a historical forecast claim inadmissible, even if that information predates final resolution. Appropriate held-out, event-grouped scores then assess the admissible forecasts; confident prose alone supplies no calibration evidence.

    Exercise 5: bag-of-words versus embeddings¶

    State one concrete limitation of bag-of-words representations that dense embeddings are specifically designed to address, and one new risk that embeddings or retrieval introduce that a bag-of-words pipeline does not have.

    Solution sketch. Bag-of-words treats synonymous or related terms ("growth", "expansion") as entirely unrelated dimensions, so a model trained on one wording may fail to generalize to a synonymous rewording; embeddings are designed to place related terms near each other in a continuous space. A new risk is that dense embeddings and retrieval components are typically produced by a separately trained model whose own training data, cutoff, and potential contamination must now also be verified — the leakage-verification burden does not disappear, it moves one level upstream.

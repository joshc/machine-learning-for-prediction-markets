    10. Evaluating probability forecasts¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Read Chapter 7, Chapter 8, and Chapter 9 first. You should be comfortable with expectation, variance, and a first derivative as a way to find a minimum, at the level of an introductory calculus-and-probability course. No prior exposure to forecast verification is assumed; every scoring identity used here is derived from these tools rather than quoted from an external theorem.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to: derive, rather than merely state, why the single-YES Brier score and natural-log loss are minimized in expectation by a truthful forecast; explain the notation registry's single-YES convention and why it differs from a two-class sum; compute log loss's infinite penalty for an exactly wrong, exactly certain forecast and explain why that is a feature, not a bug; distinguish calibration, sharpness, and discrimination as separate properties of a forecasting system; derive a reliability-resolution-uncertainty identity and show, with a worked numerical counterexample, why it holds exactly only under a grouped-forecast convention and only approximately when applied to a post hoc binned summary of individual forecasts; use a base rate and a market price as baselines without assuming either is beaten; and build a simple, self-derived, dependence-aware adjustment to naive uncertainty estimates for repeated event snapshots.

    10.1 What a proper scoring rule promises¶

    A scoring rule assigns a numeric penalty to a probability forecast once the outcome is known. This book calls a scoring rule proper for a stated class of forecasts if, when a forecaster's true belief about the outcome distribution is \(p^*\), reporting \(p=p^*\) minimizes the forecaster's own expected penalty under that belief. It is strictly proper if that minimizer is unique. This is a purely mathematical statement about incentives inside the scoring exercise. It says nothing about whether \(p^*\) was itself a good model of the world, and it says nothing about whether a market price already embedded a better estimate than the forecaster's own. Keeping the incentive property and the real-world question separate is the discipline this whole chapter is built around.

    Every propriety argument below is derived independently, from an expectation and a derivative, rather than quoted from an external theorem; the two derivations are this chapter's own, so that the argument stands on its own algebra even where a cited source goes further. Gneiting and Raftery's article on strictly proper scoring rules is the standard foundational treatment of the general theory this section specializes to the binary case, and this book's evidence ledger has now directly inspected its abstract, Section 1, Section 2.1's definitions, Section 2.3, and Sections 3.1-3.2, including its Theorem 2, Examples 1 and 3, and its categorical representation, though not every one of the article's twenty pages or proofs. GNEITING2007 Two details from that inspected text matter directly here. First, the article states its propriety results as rewards to be maximized, the opposite orientation from this chapter's losses to be minimized; every citation below silently reverses that sign convention rather than importing it unchanged. Second, and more importantly for Section 10.3, the article's own categorical squared-error representation sums an error term for every outcome category, which for a binary event is exactly twice this book's single-YES convention, a fact this chapter can now attribute to inspected text rather than only to its own derivation. Strict propriety, the inspected text is also careful to note, is an expectation-level incentive property; it does not by itself guarantee finite-sample calibration or a profitable decision once execution costs are introduced, and an outcome-dependent normalized skill score built on top of a proper score need not remain proper itself. Brier's original 1950 paper remains the historical origin of the quadratic probability score used throughout this book, but it remains a metadata-only record in this evidence ledger after repeated retrieval attempts failed; no specific theorem, numbering, or original formula from Brier's own text is attributed here beyond that bibliographic pointer. BRIER1950

    10.2 Deriving that the single-YES Brier score rewards honesty¶

    Let \(Y\) be the realized binary outcome, with \(\Pr(Y=1)=p^*\) under the forecaster's own belief, and let \(p\in[0,1]\) be whatever probability the forecaster chooses to report for \(Y=1\). Define the single-observation Brier penalty as \((p-Y)^2\). Its expectation under the forecaster's own belief is

    \[ \mathbb E[(p-Y)^2]=p^*(1-p)^2+(1-p^*)(0-p)^2 . \]

    Expand both terms: \(p^*(1-p)^2=p^*-2p^*p+p^*p^2\), and \((1-p^*)p^2=p^2-p^*p^2\). Adding them, the \(p^*p^2\) terms cancel, leaving

    \[ \mathbb E[(p-Y)^2]=p^*-2p^*p+p^2. \]

    Differentiate with respect to the reported \(p\): the derivative is \(-2p^*+2p\). Setting it to zero gives \(p=p^*\), and the second derivative is \(2\), which is strictly positive, confirming a unique minimum, not a maximum or a saddle point. In plain language: whatever the forecaster genuinely believes about \(p^*\), the expected single-YES Brier penalty is smallest exactly when the reported number matches that belief, and it is smallest by a strict margin, not merely tied with some other report. This is a complete, self-contained derivation of strict propriety for this particular scoring rule; it required nothing beyond expanding a square and taking one derivative.

    10.3 Why single-YES, not twice that¶

    A different, also common convention scores both the YES and NO probabilities and sums their squared errors: \((p-Y)^2+((1-p)-(1-Y))^2\). Since \((1-p)-(1-Y)=Y-p=-(p-Y)\), this second term equals \((p-Y)^2\) as well, so the two-class sum is exactly \(2(p-Y)^2\), twice the single-YES version. Both conventions are proper scoring rules, by the same argument as above up to a positive constant multiplier, which does not change where the expected penalty is minimized. This is not only this chapter's own algebra: the inspected categorical representation in Gneiting and Raftery's Section 3.1 sums an error term over every outcome category, which collapses to exactly this same doubled two-class sum for a binary event, confirming from directly inspected text that the categorical convention and this book's single-YES convention differ by precisely a factor of two, not by some other unstated amount. GNEITING2007 But the two conventions report different numbers for the same forecasts, and mixing them silently in one manuscript would make a stated score meaningless without knowing which convention produced it. This book's registry fixes the single-YES convention: one squared YES-probability error per binary event, not a doubled two-class sum, and every reported Brier score in this book, in the companion's scoring.brier_score, and in every worked exercise below follows that convention consistently.

    10.4 Deriving that natural-log loss rewards honesty, and its infinite penalty¶

    Define the single-observation log loss as \(-Y\log p-(1-Y)\log(1-p)\), using the natural logarithm. Its expectation under the forecaster's belief \(p^*\) is

    \[ \mathbb E[-Y\log p-(1-Y)\log(1-p)]=-p^*\log p-(1-p^*)\log(1-p). \]

    Differentiate with respect to \(p\): \(\dfrac{d}{dp}\left(-p^*\log p-(1-p^*)\log(1-p)\right)=-\dfrac{p^*}{p}+\dfrac{1-p^*}{1-p}\). Setting this to zero and multiplying through by \(p(1-p)\) gives \(-p^*(1-p)+(1-p^*)p=0\), which simplifies to \(-p^*+p^*p+p-p^*p=0\), and then to \(p=p^*\). The second derivative, \(\dfrac{p^*}{p^2}+\dfrac{1-p^*}{(1-p)^2}\), is strictly positive whenever \(p^*\) is strictly between zero and one, confirming a unique minimum at \(p=p^*\). Natural-log loss is therefore also a strictly proper scoring rule for interior beliefs, derived here the same elementary way as the Brier score: expand, differentiate, check the sign of the second derivative.

    Now consider what happens at the boundary. If a forecaster reports \(p=0\) and the outcome turns out to be \(Y=1\), the loss is \(-\log(0)\), which is not a large finite number; it is unbounded, and this book follows the natural-log convention of recording it as infinite rather than silently substituting a small clipped probability. The companion's bayes_scoring.py example computes exactly this case and confirms it directly: log_loss([1], [0]) returns inf, verified by running the shipped example in this book's own environment. By contrast, log_loss([0, 1], [0, 1]), a perfectly confident forecast that happens to be exactly correct on both observations, returns 0.0, using the ordinary calculus limit that \(y\log y\to 0\) as \(y\to 0^+\) so that a correct certain forecast contributes nothing to the loss. The asymmetry is deliberate and important: log loss punishes confident wrongness without any upper bound, while confident correctness costs nothing, which is exactly the incentive a disciplined forecaster wants, since it makes issuing an extreme probability a claim that must be backed by genuine certainty rather than a way to game an average score.

    10.5 Calibration, sharpness, and discrimination are different questions¶

    A forecasting system can fail, or succeed, along at least three distinct axes, and conflating them is one of the most common mistakes in reading a scoring result.

    Calibration asks whether, among all the cases assigned a particular probability, the outcome actually occurs at close to that rate. A system that always reports the same base rate, say 0.30 for every event in a population where 30 percent of events actually occur, is perfectly calibrated in that narrow population sense: 30 percent of its 0.30-labeled cases really do occur. It is also nearly useless for distinguishing individual cases from one another.

    Sharpness concerns concentration of each predictive distribution, independently of the realized outcome. For a Bernoulli forecast, \(p(1-p)\) measures dispersion: probabilities near zero or one are sharper than probabilities near one-half. Sharpness is not distance from a base rate. A constant 0.01 forecast is sharp even though it offers no ranking of individual cases.

    Discrimination asks whether a system actually separates cases that go on to occur from cases that do not, regardless of whether its stated numbers are calibrated. A system that ranks events correctly by their true likelihood, while being poorly calibrated on the numeric scale it reports, has good discrimination but bad calibration.

    The always-base-rate example shows why these axes are not redundant: it can be calibrated without discrimination, and its sharpness depends on how close that base rate is to zero or one. A confidently wrong system can be sharp while miscalibrated and poorly discriminating. A useful forecasting system seeks sharpness subject to calibration and checks discrimination separately; none of the three alone is a complete report card.

    Post-hoc recalibration in the companion, and where the literature sits¶

    A model's raw output need not be calibrated even when it discriminates well. The companion fits an IsotonicRegression map to boosted-model predictions and labels on the validation cohort, separate from the training cohort used to fit the trees. The resulting boosted_calibrated forecasts are then evaluated on the test cohort. Validation is the calibrator's fitting data, not its independent performance check.

    The literature motivates assessment rather than automatic correction. Guo and colleagues' inspected ICML abstract reports miscalibration and useful temperature scaling on studied image/document classifiers, not a guarantee for small event-market samples. GUO2017 Kull and colleagues' inspected abstract warns that isotonic calibration can overfit small samples and logistic calibration can worsen some probabilities; beta calibration is their proposed alternative, not an implemented companion method. KULL2017 The inspected sections of Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana discuss sigmoid and isotonic maps, independent calibration examples, and model- and sample-dependent benefits and harms. Their eight benchmark datasets used random samples, not the event-grouped chronological design needed here. NICULESCU2005

    None establishes a universally best method. An isotonic map can emit exact zero or one, incurring infinite log loss if a later outcome contradicts that certainty. In this toy run the observed test losses are finite but worse after calibration. That result is consistent with small-sample overfitting; the score comparison alone does not identify its cause or prove the same method would underperform on another sample.

    10.6 An exact grouped-score identity, and why binning changes the score¶

    There is a classical way to decompose an average Brier score into a calibration-related term, a discrimination-related term, and an irreducible term tied only to the base rate. This book derives its own version of that identity from scratch, rather than attributing a specific formula to Murphy's 1973 paper, whose full text this book's evidence ledger has not inspected beyond its bibliographic record. MURPHY1973

    Suppose observations are grouped into \(K\) bins, indexed \(k=1,\dots,K\), with \(n_k\) observations in bin \(k\), and suppose, for this derivation only, that every observation in bin \(k\) is assigned the same reported forecast \(\bar p_k\), the bin's representative value. Write \(\bar o_k\) for the empirical outcome rate within bin \(k\), and \(\bar o\) for the overall outcome rate across all \(n=\sum_k n_k\) observations. For any single observation \(i\) in bin \(k\), since its own forecast equals \(\bar p_k\) by assumption, \((\bar p_k-y_i)^2=(\bar p_k-\bar o_k)^2+(y_i-\bar o_k)^2+2(\bar p_k-\bar o_k)(\bar o_k-y_i)\); summing over \(i\) within bin \(k\), the cross term vanishes because \(\sum_i(\bar o_k-y_i)=n_k\bar o_k-n_k\bar o_k=0\). Because \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\), \(\sum_i(y_i-\bar o_k)^2=n_k\bar o_k(1-\bar o_k)\), the usual variance of a Bernoulli sample. Summing over all bins and dividing by \(n\) gives

    \[ \mathrm{BS}=\frac1n\sum_k n_k(\bar p_k-\bar o_k)^2+\frac1n\sum_k n_k\,\bar o_k(1-\bar o_k). \]

    The first term is the reliability term, zero exactly when every bin's reported forecast equals its own realized rate. The second term can itself be split, using the standard identity that total outcome variance equals within-group variance plus between-group variance: \(\frac1n\sum_k n_k\bar o_k(1-\bar o_k)=\bar o(1-\bar o)-\frac1n\sum_k n_k(\bar o_k-\bar o)^2\). Substituting gives the full decomposition

    \[ \mathrm{BS}=\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_k n_k(\bar p_k-\bar o_k)^2}_{\text{reliability}}-\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_k n_k(\bar o_k-\bar o)^2}_{\text{resolution}}+\underbrace{\bar o(1-\bar o)}_{\text{uncertainty}}. \]

    Reliability should be small and resolution large, since it is subtracted. The uncertainty term is the score of the constant empirical base-rate forecast, not a lower bound on achievable score. A perfect forecast has zero reliability and resolution equal to uncertainty, giving total score zero. The term itself is fixed by this outcome sample; an informative forecast can improve on it through resolution.

    The derivation above depended on one specific assumption: that every observation in a bin is literally assigned the bin's representative forecast \(\bar p_k\). That assumption holds exactly for an old-style categorical forecaster who genuinely issues one of \(K\) discrete probability categories. It does not generally hold when a continuous, individually varying forecast is only summarized into bins after the fact for a calibration diagnostic, which is exactly what the companion's scoring.calibration_bins function does: it reports each bin's mean probability and observed rate for display, without ever claiming that the raw Brier score computed from the original individual forecasts equals the reliability-resolution-uncertainty formula applied to those bin summaries.

    A concrete counterexample makes the gap tangible. Take \(p=(0.1,0.7,0.4,0.8)\) and \(y=(0,1,0,1)\), as in bayes_scoring.py. Their raw single-YES Brier is \(0.075\). Define a low bin by \(p<0.5\), containing \((0.1,0)\) and \((0.4,0)\), and a high bin containing \((0.7,1)\) and \((0.8,1)\). Their mean forecasts are 0.25 and 0.75 and observed rates zero and one. The bin-summary identity gives reliability \(0.0625\), resolution \(0.25\), and uncertainty \(0.25\), combining to \(0.0625\), not \(0.075\). If forecasts were actually replaced by the bin means in the original observation order, they would be \((0.25,0.75,0.25,0.75)\) and their raw score would indeed be \(0.0625\).

    The missing terms can be derived by writing \(p_i=\bar p_k+d_i\) within bin \(k\), where \(\sum_{i\in k}d_i=0\). Expanding the square gives

    \[ \mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{raw}}-\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{bin\ means}} =\frac1n\sum_k\sum_{i\in k} \left[d_i^2-2d_i(y_i-\bar o_k)\right]. \]

    Thus within-bin forecast variance and forecast-outcome covariance matter. In this particular example, outcomes are constant within each bin, so the covariance term is zero; the gap is entirely \((0.15^2+0.15^2+0.05^2+0.05^2)/4=0.0125\). Other samples can have a nonzero covariance term and a gap of either sign, as Exercise 3 shows. No approximation is needed for the grouped-score identity itself; the mistake is applying it to a different set of individually varying forecasts.

    The lesson is not that binned calibration summaries are useless; they are a legitimate and standard diagnostic. The lesson is that a population-level identity derived under a grouped-forecast assumption is not automatically an exact statement about a raw, continuously varying, individual-level score, and a report that quotes "reliability" and "resolution" numbers computed from bins must say so plainly rather than implying they reconstruct the underlying Brier score exactly.

    The same calibration_bins function, run on the logistic model's actual 45-event test-cohort forecasts in examples\forecast_models.py, illustrates the diagnostic at a more realistic scale: four bins contain 3, 23, 16, and 3 observations respectively, with mean forecasts of 0.212908, 0.393523, 0.626452, and 0.809405 against observed rates of 0.333333, 0.391304, 0.6875, and 1.0. Read as a diagnostic, the middle two bins, holding 39 of the 45 events, track their own mean forecast reasonably closely, while the two edge bins, each with only three observations, show larger gaps that a three-observation sample cannot pin down precisely. Reporting this table is useful for judging where a model's stated probabilities are and are not trustworthy; treating its bin-level reliability and resolution as an exact recovery of the model's already-reported raw Brier score of 0.198372 would repeat exactly the conflation this section has just shown, with the hand-checked four-point example, to be unjustified in general.

    10.7 Baselines: a base rate and a market price are not automatically beaten¶

    A forecast score is only informative relative to a stated comparison. This book insists on at least two baselines wherever a model score is reported. The first is the overall base rate: the unconditional event frequency in the relevant training population, exactly the reference_rate field the companion's FittedForecaster.predict method reports alongside its model outputs, computed as the mean training-cohort outcome. The second, when available, is the market price itself, treated as a forecasting benchmark rather than an executable quote, exactly the market_probability field the same method reports.

    Neither baseline is a strawman to be casually outscored. The inspected NBER working-paper abstract by Wolfers and Zitzewitz gives sufficient conditions under which an equilibrium market price reflects an average belief about an event's probability, while explicitly discussing biased approximations outside those conditions; this book has inspected only that abstract, not the paper's full derivations, so it is cited here as a reading pointer for why a market price deserves to be treated as a serious probabilistic estimate, not as a certified proof that any particular market price equals the true event probability. WZ2006 A model that beats the base rate but not the market price has shown it captures some structure the base rate misses; it has not shown it captures anything the market did not already know. A model that beats neither has provided no evidence of anything beyond its own internal consistency. Reporting a model's score without reporting both of these comparisons, on the same predeclared evaluation cohort, invites exactly the kind of unearned impressiveness this book has repeatedly warned against.

    Running examples\forecast_models.py on the fixed 45-event test cohort reports single-YES Briers: base rate 0.249699; invented market benchmark 0.207439; logistic 0.198372; boosted 0.220607; validation-calibrated boosted 0.2672; and fixed equal-weight logistic/calibrated-boosted blend 0.224694. Logistic beats both baselines on this sample; boosted beats the base rate but not the market benchmark; calibration worsens the boosted model's score here. These are observed synthetic results from one seed, not general model rankings or market-beating evidence. Small-sample overfitting is a plausible explanation for the calibration deterioration, consistent with the literature's warning, but not a cause established by this one comparison. KULL2017

    10.8 Population versus sample: what should \(n\) mean¶

    The Brier score and log loss defined above as expectations are population quantities: they describe the forecaster's own belief distribution exactly, with no sampling involved. Any number actually computed from data, including every number in Sections 10.6 and 10.7, is a sample estimate of some population quantity, subject to sampling variability that shrinks only as genuinely new, independent information accumulates. Reporting a sample average without acknowledging that it estimates, rather than equals, an underlying population score is a common and avoidable overstatement.

    The notation registry is explicit that \(n\) should count evaluation observations, with the number of independent or dependence-grouped events reported separately. This chapter's next two sections show why that separation matters in practice: raw row counts routinely overstate how much independent information a dataset actually contains.

    10.9 Repeated quotes on one outcome are not independent evaluation observations¶

    A dataset with 360 rows from 180 events contains 180 realized outcomes, not 360 independent outcomes. Scoring all snapshots can nevertheless be legitimate if the estimand is average performance across specified horizons and the uncertainty calculation respects grouping. Reporting \(n=360\) is then a row count, not an independence claim; report 180 event groups alongside it and explain the weights. The companion instead chooses one latest predeclared snapshot per event. evaluate_forecaster applies latest_per_event to the supplied cohort, yielding 45 test events in the default run, not the full fixture's 180 events. This answers a latest-horizon question and deliberately does not validate all-horizon performance.

    10.10 A temporal gap does not cure a shared label, restated for evaluation¶

    The same principle from Chapters 8 and 9 applies once more, now specifically to scoring rather than to splitting or exploring: if a training cohort and an evaluation cohort share a dependence group, the fact that their decision timestamps are far apart does not make their scores independent contributions to different questions. grouped_chronological_split's purging of straddling groups exists precisely so that the score computed on a later cohort reflects genuinely unseen dependence groups, not merely temporally later rows drawn from an already-seen event.

    A closely related requirement is that training and calibration labels must actually have been available by their own cohort's cutoff before being used to fit or calibrate anything scored afterward. The companion's fit_forecaster and its internal _validate_split check enforce this directly: they raise an error if any cohort's rows have a label available after that cohort's own cutoff, or if any event or dependence group appears in more than one cohort. A reported evaluation score is only as trustworthy as the guarantee that nothing scored on the test cohort quietly informed, through a shared label or a shared group, any earlier fitting or calibration step.

    10.11 A worked design-effect derivation for dependence-aware uncertainty¶

    Section 10.9 established that raw row counts can overstate independent information; this section derives, from first principles, one simple way to quantify that overstatement, then points to more rigorous established alternatives for a serious analysis.

    Suppose an evaluation sample consists of \(G\) dependence groups (events), each contributing exactly \(m\) observations (snapshots), for a total of \(n=Gm\) rows. Suppose, for this illustrative derivation, that any two observations within the same group share a common correlation \(\rho\) in whatever quantity is being averaged, for example a per-observation score contribution, and that observations in different groups are uncorrelated, each with variance \(\sigma^2\). The variance of the sum of all \(n\) observations is the sum of \(n\) individual variances, \(n\sigma^2\), plus the sum of all within-group covariance pairs. Each group of size \(m\) has \(m(m-1)\) ordered pairs, each contributing covariance \(\rho\sigma^2\), and there are \(G\) such groups, giving total covariance \(Gm(m-1)\rho\sigma^2\). The variance of the grand mean is this total variance divided by \(n^2=(Gm)^2\):

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{Gm\sigma^2+Gm(m-1)\rho\sigma^2}{(Gm)^2}=\frac{\sigma^2\left[1+(m-1)\rho\right]}{Gm}. \]

    A naive calculation that assumed all \(n=Gm\) observations were independent would instead compute \(\sigma^2/(Gm)\), understating the true variance by the factor \(1+(m-1)\rho\) whenever \(\rho>0\). Equivalently, the effective sample size is \(n_{\text{eff}}=n/[1+(m-1)\rho]\), the number of genuinely independent observations that would produce the same variance as the actual dependent sample.

    Apply this to the recurring-event fixture's structure: \(m=2\) snapshots per event, \(G=180\) events, \(n=360\) rows. Take an invented, clearly hypothetical value \(\rho=0.5\), chosen only to illustrate the arithmetic and not measured from any real evaluation: the design-effect factor is \(1+(2-1)(0.5)=1.5\), and the effective sample size is \(n_{\text{eff}}=360/1.5=240\), well below the naive row count of 360 but, reassuringly, also comfortably above the 180 realized events, since two positively correlated but not perfectly identical snapshots still carry more information together than either alone. If \(\rho\) were instead 1, perfect redundancy, the effective sample size would collapse exactly to 180, matching the number of realized events, exactly as the extreme case in Chapter 9 anticipated.

    This derivation is a simple, self-contained heuristic, not a substitute for a properly justified dependence model. Two more rigorous tools are worth knowing as further reading. The stationary bootstrap of Politis and Romano is a resampling method designed for dependent time-series data, using randomly chosen block lengths to preserve dependence structure under stated stationarity assumptions. This book's evidence ledger has inspected the complete primary publisher abstract, which describes geometrically distributed random block lengths and a conditionally stationary resampled pseudo-series, developed for asymptotic inference under weak dependence and stationarity; it has not inspected the original article's full proofs or a specific recommended block-length choice, and Chapter 9's inspection of White's related reality-check construction supplies a more detailed, separately verified exposition of a closely related common-random-length block bootstrap. POLITIS1994 Neither source should be read as guaranteeing confidence-interval validity for nonstationary data or for an arbitrarily chosen dependence unit; naming a bootstrap is not a substitute for correctly specifying the sampling unit, exactly as this section's own design-effect derivation had to specify its event-versus-row unit explicitly rather than assume it. Howard, Ramdas, McAuliffe, and Sekhon's work on time-uniform, nonparametric confidence sequences, whose abstract and relevant Section 2 setup this book's evidence ledger did inspect, including its average-conditional-expectation estimand and exponential-supermartingale construction, offers a genuinely different guarantee: simultaneous-in-time coverage that remains valid even if a researcher looks at the running estimate repeatedly, rather than only at one predetermined sample size. HOWARD2021 That property is attractive for the kind of repeated confirmatory temptation Chapter 9 warned against, but it is not a license to ignore dependence; a time-uniform guarantee is not automatically an assumption-free one, and it does not by itself turn a shared-event leak into an independent observation.

    10.12 Accuracy is not edge¶

    Every derivation and worked example in this chapter concerns forecast quality: how well a probability matches an outcome, evaluated by a rule constructed so that honesty is the best policy. None of it is a statement about trading profitability. A model can achieve an excellent Brier score, beat the base rate convincingly, and even edge out the market price on a predeclared, dependence-aware, properly grouped evaluation, and still offer no positive expected trading value once executable asks and bids, fees, capital lockup, and adverse selection are accounted for, because none of those costs and frictions appear anywhere in a Brier score or a log loss computation. Conversely, a genuinely well-calibrated forecast can be scored favorably while telling a trader nothing new, if the market price already embedded the same information; a favorable score relative to the base rate is not automatically a favorable score relative to the market, and only the latter comparison starts to speak to whether a model contributes information beyond what was already priced. Evaluating a forecast and evaluating a trading decision are related but distinct exercises, and this book keeps them in separate chapters for that reason: this chapter is about the first, and the decision-and-cost machinery of the earlier chapters, revisited with execution realism in Part 4, is about the second.

    Assumptions and failure modes¶

    Assumption: the reported probability is genuinely the forecaster's belief, not a strategically shaded number. Failure mode: propriety only rewards honesty if nothing external, such as a scoring-based bonus structure with its own distortions, incentivizes misreporting; this chapter's derivations assume the forecaster is simply trying to minimize expected penalty under their own belief.

    Assumption: a binned calibration summary is an exact decomposition of the raw score. Failure mode: as Section 10.6 showed by direct computation, it generally is not, unless forecasts are genuinely constant within each bin; treating the two as interchangeable overstates precision.

    Assumption: beating a base rate is evidence of a tradable edge. Failure mode: it is evidence only that a model captures some structure a constant forecast misses; it says nothing about whether that structure was already reflected in an executable price.

    Assumption: raw row count is the right sample size for uncertainty. Failure mode: repeated snapshots of one event inflate the apparent sample size and understate true uncertainty, as the design-effect derivation in Section 10.11 quantifies.

    Assumption: a wide date range in an evaluation cohort guarantees temporal stability. Failure mode: pooling a long evaluation window into one summary score can hide a structural change partway through; grouping evaluation by calendar period, for example by quarter, and inspecting whether scores are stable across those periods, is a useful complement to a single pooled number, though small per-period counts can themselves be noisy and should not be over-interpreted individually.

    Exercises and solution sketches¶

    Exercise 1: deriving the minimizer directly¶

    Using the expansion in Section 10.2, verify by direct substitution that reporting \(p=p^*\) gives expected Brier penalty \(p^*(1-p^*)\), and explain in words why this quantity can never be negative and is largest at \(p^*=0.5\).

    Solution sketch. Substituting \(p=p^*\) into \(p^*-2p^*p+p^2\) gives \(p^*-2(p^*)^2+(p^*)^2=p^*-(p^*)^2=p^*(1-p^*)\). This is the variance of a single Bernoulli draw, which is never negative, and it is maximized at \(p^*=0.5\) because that is where an outcome is hardest to anticipate; a forecaster facing a coin-flip-like event cannot achieve as low an expected penalty as one facing a near-certain event, no matter how honestly they report.

    Exercise 2: the cost of an overconfident wrong forecast¶

    A forecaster reports \(p=0.02\) for an event that occurs. Compute the natural-log loss, and contrast it with the loss if the same forecaster had instead reported the base rate for that population, \(p=0.30\).

    Solution sketch. The loss for the overconfident wrong report is \(-\log(0.02)\approx 3.91\). The loss for reporting the base rate instead is \(-\log(0.30)\approx 1.20\). Confidently betting against an outcome that occurs costs roughly three times as much as simply reporting the base rate would have, illustrating why unjustified sharpness is penalized heavily under log loss.

    Exercise 3: reproducing the decomposition gap¶

    Using the bin definitions in Section 10.6, recompute reliability, resolution, and uncertainty if the "low" bin's two observations are \(p=0.2,y=0\) and \(p=0.3,y=1\), keeping the "high" bin unchanged. Compare the decomposition's combined value to the true raw Brier score for these four observations, and state whether they still match.

    Solution sketch. The raw Brier score is \(\tfrac14[(0.2-0)^2+(0.3-1)^2+(0.7-1)^2+(0.8-1)^2]=\tfrac14[0.04+0.49+0.09+0.04]=0.165\). The low bin's mean forecast is \(0.25\) and its observed rate is \(0.5\) (one of its two observations occurred), giving reliability contribution \(2(0.25-0.5)^2=0.125\); the high bin is unchanged at \(2(0.75-1)^2=0.125\); total reliability is \(0.25/4=0.0625\). Three of the four observations now occur in total, so the overall rate is \(\bar o=0.75\), giving uncertainty \(0.75(1-0.75)=0.1875\). The low bin's resolution term is \(2(0.5-0.75)^2=0.125\) and the high bin's is \(2(1-0.75)^2=0.125\), giving resolution \(0.25/4=0.0625\). Combined: \(0.0625-0.0625+0.1875=0.1875\), which does not equal the raw score of \(0.165\); the two again differ by \(0.0225\), reinforcing that the match in Section 10.6's grouped case was a property of that specific constant-within-bin construction, not a general guarantee.

    Exercise 4: computing an effective sample size¶

    An evaluation set has \(G=90\) events with \(m=4\) snapshots each. Assuming a within-event correlation of \(\rho=0.3\), compute the design-effect factor and the effective sample size, and compare it to the naive row count.

    Solution sketch. The design-effect factor is \(1+(4-1)(0.3)=1.9\). The naive row count is \(n=360\). The effective sample size is \(360/1.9\approx 189.5\), far closer to the 90 realized events than to the 360 raw rows, illustrating that even a modest within-event correlation sharply reduces the amount of independent evaluation information a larger snapshot count appears to offer.

    Exercise 5: base rate, market, and model together¶

    A model reports a Brier score of 0.18 on a predeclared test cohort where the base-rate benchmark scores 0.21 and the market-price benchmark scores 0.16. What, if anything, can be concluded about the model's tradable value?

    Solution sketch. The model beats the base rate, showing it captures some structure a constant forecast misses, but it does not beat the market price, so there is no evidence here that it adds information beyond what was already reflected in executable prices. Nothing in this comparison addresses fees, executable spreads, capital lockup, or dependence-aware uncertainty around these three numbers; a defensible conclusion is limited to relative forecast quality on this specific cohort, not a claim of tradable edge.

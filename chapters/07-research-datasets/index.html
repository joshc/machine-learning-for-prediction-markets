    7. Designing the research dataset¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Read Part 1, especially the distinction between an informal event and a contractual instrument (Chapter 1), probability as a modeled quantity rather than an oracle (Chapter 2), and rules, venues, and settlement risk (Chapter 6). This chapter assumes you can read a schema table, reason about set membership, and compute a weighted average by hand. It does not assume prior experience with data engineering.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to: separate an event family, an event instance, a venue contract, an outcome token, and a rule version into distinct identifiers; explain why a non-binary or exceptional payout must be represented before it is coerced into a YES/NO label; design a predeclared selection rule that keeps unresolved and excluded records visible instead of silently dropped; and state, for a candidate dataset, which of five provenance categories it belongs to: an invented synthetic fixture, a real retrospective extract, a live market snapshot, a genuinely prospective collection, or an authorized exchange archive. You should also be able to explain why a licensing gap is a research-design constraint, not an afterthought to resolve once collection is finished.

    7.1 Four things a headline conflates¶

    A market's short title is a compressed description of something the book already warned against treating as self-explanatory. Before any statistical work can begin, a research dataset needs to separate at least four distinct concepts that a headline collapses into one sentence.

    An event family is a recurring question template: the same kind of proposition, asked repeatedly with a similar structure, such as "will the recorded monthly value exceed the previous month's recorded value." A family is a design convenience for pooling related observations. It is not evidence that every instance behaves identically, and treating family membership as automatic independence is a mistake this book returns to repeatedly.

    An event instance is one concrete realization of that family tied to a specific calendar period or occurion, for example the specific comparison between one named month and its predecessor. A contract is a particular venue's tradable object that references an event instance: it has its own rules text, its own resolution source, its own token identifiers, and potentially its own effective dates, even when two contracts on two venues appear to ask the same informal question. An outcome token is the specific tradable side of a contract, such as its YES leg or one option within a categorical event; a binary contract ordinarily has two complementary tokens, while a categorical event can have several, and the token identifier is what actually trades, not the informal outcome description.

    Finally, a rule version is a specific, dated statement of the resolution procedure. Rules can be clarified or amended after a contract begins trading. The inspected Polymarket resolution documentation describes an additional-context clarification process and an exceptional Unknown/50-50 outcome in which each token redeems at 0.50 rather than a binary 0/1 payout. POLYMARKET_RESOLUTION That single documented fact already tells you two design requirements: the payout space for a contract is not always {0, 1}, and the resolution text that governs a particular historical decision must be the version that was in force at that time, not whatever version happens to be currently displayed.

    7.2 Why the separation is not academic¶

    Consider a concrete, if invented, scenario to see why conflating these identifiers damages a study rather than merely complicating its paperwork. Suppose a researcher is building a dataset of "will candidate X be certified as the winner in region Y" questions. Two different venues each list a contract for what looks like the same underlying election. If the dataset assigns each venue's contract its own independent event identifier, an evaluation that reports one score per row will implicitly treat the two venues' realized outcomes as two independent pieces of evidence about the world. They are not: both contracts typically resolve from the same underlying authoritative announcement. A single certification event produces two correlated contractual labels, not two independent draws.

    The fix is not to discard one of the two contracts. It is to record a dependence-group identifier that both contracts share, distinct from either contract's own identifier, so that any later evaluation or resampling procedure can treat them as one cluster of dependent observations rather than as two independent rows. The companion's EventRow schema, examined in the next chapter, keeps event_id, group_id, and family_id as three separate fields for exactly this reason: its synthetic fixture happens to set group_id equal to event_id because each invented event has only one realized outcome represented by two feature snapshots of the same event, but a real cross-venue dataset would need group_id to span multiple contract identifiers whenever they share an underlying settlement source. Recording this distinction at schema-design time is cheap. Reconstructing it after the fact, once two years of rows have already been logged with only a flat contract identifier, is often impossible, because the information linking the two venues' contracts to a shared settlement event may not have been retained anywhere in the collected data.

    A parallel conflation happens with rule versions. If a dataset stores only "the current rules text" alongside a historical row, and that text is later edited after a clarification, the dataset silently rewrites history: a contract that traded for six months under one resolution definition now appears, in the stored data, to have always operated under the amended definition. The remedy is to store a rule-version identifier and its effective timestamp alongside every contract row, and to change that identifier only when the underlying text changes, never retroactively.

    7.3 The non-binary payout, worked from first principles¶

    Say a category-style contract can resolve to one of three payouts: one dollar, forty cents, or zero, depending on which of three documented resolution branches applies. Write \(R\) for the realized per-unit payout and \(\mathcal R=\{0,\,0.40,\,1\}\) for its possible values. A model assigns probabilities \(P(R=1)=0.52\), \(P(R=0.40)=0.18\), and \(P(R=0)=0.30\); these three numbers must sum to one, and they do here: \(0.52+0.18+0.30=1.00\).

    The expected payout is the probability-weighted sum over the payout set:

    \[ \mathbb E[R]=\sum_{r\in\mathcal R} r\,P(R=r)=1(0.52)+0.40(0.18)+0(0.30)=0.52+0.072=0.592. \]

    Now compare two ways of building a "binary classification target" from this same contract. The careless approach collapses \(R\) into \(Y=\mathbf 1\{R>0\}\), treating the forty-cent branch as an ordinary YES. Under that coding, \(P(Y=1)=0.52+0.18=0.70\), and a classifier trained on \(Y\) would be predicting a quantity that no longer corresponds to any tradable payout: a position bought at an ask of USD 0.65 would look attractive against a 0.70 YES probability, yet the actual expected payout is USD 0.592, and the arithmetic edge the naive framing suggested (0.70 - 0.65 = 0.05) has nothing to do with the true expected settlement value (0.592 - 0.65 = -0.058, before any fee). The disciplined approach keeps \(R\) as the labeled quantity, models its full distribution over \(\mathcal R\), and only forms a binary label when a specific research question genuinely calls for one, documenting the coding rule explicitly rather than assuming it away.

    This is not a claim that categorical contracts are common or that this specific 0.52/0.18/0.30 split describes any actual market; the numbers are invented to make the arithmetic concrete. The lesson transfers directly to the documented Polymarket exception: if a dataset simply assigns \(Y=1\) to every settled-YES row and \(Y=0\) to every settled-NO row, an Unknown/50-50 resolution has no honest binary code. Recording it as either 0 or 1 manufactures a label that never existed; the schema needs a place to keep the actual payout value, a way to flag it as exceptional, and a decision, made in advance of any analysis, about whether such rows are excluded, retained as a separate multiclass target, or converted into an appropriately weighted training instance.

    7.4 Selection: who gets to be a row¶

    A dataset does not assemble itself. A researcher chooses an eligibility rule, and that choice is itself part of the empirical claim. Suppose the intended population is "all contracts within event family \(F\) that began trading between two fixed dates." A naive collection strategy queries a live listing endpoint at the end of that window and keeps whatever contracts still appear. This strategy silently excludes any contract that was already delisted, cancelled, or moved to a historical-only endpoint before the query ran; the documented Kalshi historical-data guide explicitly warns that live and historical partitions are governed by moving cutoffs and object-specific fields, so a query against only the live surface at one point in time is not equivalent to a complete historical listing. KALSHI_HISTORY The glossary calls this failure mode survivorship bias, and it applies as much to a dataset-construction script as it does to a trading backtest.

    A workable predeclared rule instead fixes the population by a stable identifying property, not by what a single query happens to return. For example: "every contract of family \(F\) whose rule text was published between the two dates, collected from both the live and historical endpoints, with an explicit record of which endpoint supplied each row." A concrete accounting example makes the bookkeeping habit explicit. Suppose 500 candidate contract identifiers are found across both endpoints. Of these, 40 are excluded because no rule text could be retrieved for any version, and a further 15 remain unresolved (no final payout recorded) as of the freeze date chosen for the study. The dataset should report all three numbers: 500 candidates, 40 excluded for missing rules, 15 retained as censored unresolved rows, and 445 retained with a realized payout. Reporting only "445 labeled contracts" without the other three numbers hides exactly the information a reader needs to judge whether the missingness could be related to the outcome being studied, for example if markets that resolve unfavorably to a popular narrative are disproportionately represented among the unresolved set at freeze time.

    Selection also interacts with licensing. A predeclared eligibility rule cannot include data a researcher is not permitted to redistribute or, in some cases, even to retain past a specified period. That constraint has to be decided before collection, not discovered afterward when a contributor tries to publish the resulting extract.

    7.5 A conceptual minimum schema¶

    The table below states a conceptual minimum for a multi-source research dataset. It is a design target, not a claim that the companion already implements every column. Its EventRow is a small recurring-event schema with distinct event, publication, observation, decision, and label-availability timestamps, but only one shared publication/observation pair for all features in a row. It does not represent every feature's separate source clock or the cross-venue, categorical-payout generality shown here. Those require additional provenance and validation.

    Field Conceptual role
    event_family_id Recurring question template; a pooling convenience, not proof of independence.
    event_instance_id One concrete calendar realization within a family.
    contract_id A specific venue's tradable object referencing the instance.
    venue The operator and product, since two venues' rules can differ even for a shared instance.
    outcome_token_id The specific tradable side actually quoted and filled.
    dependence_group_id Shared identifier across contracts/instances tied to one underlying settlement source.
    rule_version_id and rule_effective_at The dated resolution text actually in force for this row.
    resolution_source The stated authority or procedure that determines the payout.
    payout_space The full set of contractually possible payout values, not assumed to be {0, 1}.
    realized_payout The actual settled value, kept distinct from any derived binary label.
    censored_flag Whether an outcome remained undetermined as of the dataset's freeze time.
    license_basis The specific permission or public-domain basis for retaining and sharing this row.

    Building every column in this table for a live venue is a substantial undertaking, and this book does not claim to have done so. What it commits to is smaller and more honest: keeping these concepts distinct in any schema you do build, and refusing to compress them prematurely for convenience.

    7.6 Provenance categories, and why this book keeps five separate ones¶

    The feasibility review behind this book's real-data chapters reached a specific, narrow conclusion: no established, small, redistributable, complete point-in-time prediction-market order-book dataset was identified. Official venue documentation is a useful interface for future prospective collection, but an accessible API is not a redistribution license, and a historical price series is not a historical order book. This book therefore keeps five provenance categories strictly apart, and every dataset or example you encounter later should be identifiable as exactly one of them.

    A synthetic fixture is intentionally invented data, generated by a documented deterministic process, used to exercise a mechanism or an algorithm. The companion's recurring-event fixture is exactly this: 180 invented events with a stated random seed, an invented logistic latent process, and an explicitly labeled synthetic=True flag on every row. It teaches point-in-time discipline and model plumbing; it says nothing about any real market.

    A real retrospective extract is genuine historical observational data, collected and frozen for a specific study, with its provenance, exclusions, and vintage limitations documented. This book's chosen example is the NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory's Mauna Loa monthly carbon-dioxide series, restricted to January 1975 through December 2025 to avoid the earlier Scripps-attributed period, with interpolation-flagged months excluded and the December 2022-July 2023 Maunakea substitution recorded as a location discontinuity. NOAA_CO2 This is real data about a real physical process. It is not prediction-market data, not an issued forecast archive, and not evidence about market prices.

    That design has since been carried through to a completed, hash-verified frozen comparison, documented in experiments/README.md: a target-calendar-month Beta(1,1) forecaster scores a single-YES Brier of about 0.032 against an expanding-overall-rate baseline's roughly 0.230 on 132 primary evaluation events (85 YES outcomes, an observed rate near 0.644), with a closely similar paired difference on a 123-event sensitivity cohort that removes the Maunakea-affected months and refits both models on the reduced admitted labels. Reporting this real, checkable number does not loosen any of this section's provenance boundaries. The underlying series remains current-vintage, not a reconstructed historical-vintage archive; its forecast-availability clock is stated plainly, in the study's own protocol, as an explicitly hypothetical retrospective convention, not a verified historical publication record; and the comparison contains no market price, no executable contract, and no claim about trading profitability. A strong, honestly reported seasonal effect in a real physical series is a genuine empirical result about that series and a fair illustration of everything this book asks a frozen confirmatory design to look like; it is not, by itself, evidence about prediction markets, and this book does not claim otherwise anywhere.

    A current market snapshot is a live read of a venue's present state, such as a single order-book query. It is useful for understanding today's mechanics and for building a prospective collector, but a single snapshot is not a time series and must never be presented as if it had been recorded historically.

    A genuinely prospective record is one collected going forward from the moment of collection, with its own observation timestamps, so that its point-in-time properties are actually true by construction rather than reconstructed after the fact. The companion now ships exactly this kind of collector as an explicitly optional module, prediction_market_lab.market_data, documented in data/README.md and in the examples' own README. It is opt-in in every sense that matters for this chapter's licensing discussion: constructing its client without an explicit acknowledge_terms=True acknowledgement fails outright, its command-line form requires the same --acknowledge-terms flag before any network call, no default example or test ever invokes it, and it acquires and commits no actual venue payload as part of this book. Its acknowledgement flag is a recorded user acknowledgement, not a legal eligibility determination, and this book still has not established an unrestricted venue-data redistribution license for either inspected venue. KALSHI_HISTORY POLYMARKET_TERMS Chapter 8 discusses, and this module's saved snapshot schema concretely implements, what such a collector needs to record; it is not a live broker, not a historical archive, and its native fields are kept entirely separate from anything PaperBroker accepts.

    An authorized exchange archive is a dataset obtained under an explicit, checked permission from a venue or data provider, covering historical activity the requester did not personally observe as it happened. This book has not obtained such an archive, and no example claims to use one. If you pursue this path yourself, the licensing question must be resolved and documented before a single row is retained, exactly as the terms-of-use gaps identified for both inspected venues make clear.     7.7 Assumptions and failure modes¶

    Every schema choice above rests on assumptions that can fail quietly.

    Assumption: a family's instances are behaviorally homogeneous. Failure mode: template drift. A recurring question's exact wording, threshold, or resolution source can shift slightly across instances even while a human reader would call them "the same kind of question." A model or evaluation that pools across a family without checking wording stability can be blindsided by a change that looks cosmetic but alters the actual payout rule.

    Assumption: an outcome token maps one-to-one onto a contract. Failure mode: shared liquidity or shared settlement across a set of tokens within one categorical event. The inspected Polymarket documentation on markets and events explicitly separates market and outcome-token identifiers from an event container, and notes that only some multi-market events are mutually exclusive; grouping by container is not proof of an exhaustive, non-overlapping partition. POLYMARKET_MARKETS

    Assumption: rule text is stable between collection and analysis. Failure mode: a documented clarification or dispute resolution changes the applicable rule after trading has already occurred under an earlier reading. Recording only "current rules" erases this history.

    Assumption: licensing can be resolved after collection. Failure mode: a dataset is built, analyzed, and prepared for release before anyone checks whether redistribution is permitted, at which point the only honest options are to discard the collection effort or restrict the release to derived, non-redistributable summaries.

    Assumption: unresolved rows can simply be dropped for convenience. Failure mode: if unresolved status correlates with the outcome under study, for example because contentious or slow-to-resolve markets are also the ones most likely to move against a popular expectation, dropping them silently biases every downstream estimate.

    Exercises and solution sketches¶

    Exercise 1: four identifiers, one headline¶

    A news aggregator lists "Will the index close above its prior close on Friday" as a single question. Two venues each offer a contract on this question, with different resolution sources (one uses a real-time feed, one uses an end-of-day settlement print). Name the four identifiers this book asks you to keep distinct here, and state which of them the two venues' contracts do and do not share.

    Solution sketch. The event family is the recurring "close above prior close" template. The event instance is this specific Friday's realization. The venues create distinct contracts and outcome tokens because their rules and sources differ. Nevertheless, both contracts depend on the same underlying Friday index movement and should be grouped for that shared exposure, or handled by an explicitly justified dependence model. Their labels need not be identical to be dependent. A dependence group is not a certificate of contractual equivalence: the different source definitions still prevent assuming a riskless hedge.

    Exercise 2: coercing a non-binary payout¶

    A contract's payout space is \(\{0, 0.30, 1\}\) with modeled probabilities 0.55, 0.10, and 0.35. Compute the expected payout. Then compute what a careless YES/NO coding with \(Y=\mathbf 1\{R>0\}\) would report as \(P(Y=1)\), and explain why the two numbers should not be compared to the same ask price.

    Solution sketch. Expected payout is \(1(0.35)+0.30(0.10)+0(0.55)=0.35+0.03=0.38\). The careless binary coding gives \(P(Y=1)=0.35+0.10=0.45\). Comparing an ask price to 0.45 answers a question about a manufactured label that does not correspond to any single tradable payout; comparing it to the true expected payout of 0.38 is the only calculation with a defensible economic interpretation, and even that comparison still needs the executable ask, applicable fees, and the model's own uncertainty before it says anything about a favorable action.

    Exercise 3: reporting selection honestly¶

    A collection effort finds 240 candidate contracts. Eighteen have no retrievable rule text at any version and are excluded. Twelve remain unresolved at the freeze date. Report the full accounting a reader would need, and explain one plausible way the unresolved group could bias a naive "resolved-only" analysis.

    Solution sketch. Report 240 candidates, 18 excluded for missing rule text, 12 retained as censored/unresolved, and 210 with a realized payout. If, for instance, the unresolved 12 are disproportionately contracts nearing a disputed or delayed resolution because the underlying event turned out to be genuinely ambiguous, dropping them could remove exactly the harder cases that a model most needs to be evaluated against, inflating apparent performance on the easier, already-resolved remainder.

    Exercise 4: naming the provenance category¶

    A student downloads a single current order-book snapshot from a venue's public API, saves it to a file, and later builds a chart from ten such snapshots taken every hour over one afternoon. Which of the five provenance categories in this chapter applies, and what must the student avoid claiming about it?

    Solution sketch. This is a genuinely prospective record: each snapshot is collected going forward from its own observation time, with real timestamps. The student must avoid presenting it as historical evidence beyond that single afternoon, avoid treating the ten snapshots as a complete order-book history (gaps exist between polls), and avoid assuming any redistribution permission beyond what the venue's terms actually grant.

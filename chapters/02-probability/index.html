    2. Probability as disciplined uncertainty¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Chapter 1 (event contracts, categorical/threshold/complement shapes, and the \(Z\)/\(Y\)/\(R\) distinction). Comfort with basic set notation and simple algebra. No prior statistics course is assumed; the chapter derives what it needs.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to: state the probability axioms and derive Bayes' rule from the definition of conditional probability; convert between probability and odds; explain what a base rate is and why it requires a stated reference population; distinguish independent from dependent events with a concrete event-contract example; and compute the single-YES Brier score and natural-log loss for a small set of forecasts exactly as this book defines them.

    2.1 Probability is a commitment, not a vibe¶

    "I think it's likely" is not a probability. A probability is a number in \([0,1]\) attached to a precisely specified event, under a precisely specified information set, that obeys a small set of consistency rules. The rules matter more than they might seem to at first, because they are what make a probability combinable: they let you take two forecasts and merge them, compare a forecast to a price, or later score a forecast against what actually happened.

    The axioms this book relies on are the ordinary Kolmogorov ones, stated for a discrete or countable outcome space (continuous cases replace sums with integrals, but nothing in this book's contract-pricing content requires that generality): every event \(A\) has \(P(A)\geq 0\); the certain event has \(P(\Omega)=1\); and for mutually exclusive events, probabilities add, \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)\) when \(A\cap B=\emptyset\). From these three rules, the complement rule \(P(A^c)=1-P(A)\) follows immediately by taking \(B=A^c\) in the additivity axiom. This is exactly why, in Chapter 1's complement contracts, YES and NO probabilities must sum to one when no third exceptional state is contractually possible — the axiom requires exhaustiveness and mutual exclusivity, and a documented "Unknown/50-50" settlement state is precisely a violation of exhaustiveness over the two-outcome frame, not a mathematical paradox.

    Conditional probability is defined, not derived, as

    \[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0. \]

    Everything else in this chapter is algebra performed on that one definition.

    2.2 Bayes' rule, derived, not quoted¶

    Bayes' rule is often presented as a black box. It is worth deriving it once, slowly, because the derivation is what tells you exactly which quantities you need and what happens when one of them is zero.

    Start from the definition of conditional probability applied twice, once for \(P(A\mid B)\) and once for \(P(B\mid A)\):

    \[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}. \]

    Both expressions share the same numerator, \(P(A\cap B)\). Solve the second equation for that shared numerator: \(P(A\cap B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)\). Substitute this into the first equation:

    \[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}. \]

    That is Bayes' rule. Nothing was assumed beyond the definition of conditional probability applied in two directions. The denominator \(P(B)\) is usually not known directly and must be expanded using the law of total probability. If \(A\) has a binary complement \(A^c\),

    \[ P(B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)+P(B\mid A^c)P(A^c). \]

    Putting the two together gives the working form used throughout this book:

    \[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B\mid A)P(A)+P(B\mid A^c)P(A^c)}. \]

    Read this equation as a recipe with three ingredients: a prior \(P(A)\) (what you believed before seeing evidence \(B\)), a likelihood ratio implicit in \(P(B\mid A)\) versus \(P(B\mid A^c)\) (how much more or less likely the evidence is under each hypothesis), and the posterior \(P(A\mid B)\) (what you believe after). If \(P(B\mid A)=P(B\mid A^c)\), the evidence is equally likely regardless of \(A\), and the posterior equals the prior exactly: evidence that does not discriminate between hypotheses cannot move your belief, no matter how dramatic it feels.

    Worked case. Take a prior \(P(A)=0.20\) that some event contract's underlying proposition is true. Suppose a piece of evidence \(B\) (say, a leaked internal report) has \(P(B\mid A)=0.80\) — quite likely if the proposition is true — and \(P(B\mid A^c)=0.10\) — fairly unlikely if it is false. The marginal probability of observing this evidence at all is

    \[ P(B)=0.20\times0.80+0.80\times0.10=0.16+0.08=0.24. \]

    The posterior is

    \[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{0.16}{0.24}=0.6\overline{6}\approx0.667. \]

    A prior of 0.20 moved to roughly 0.667 after one piece of evidence that was eight times more likely under the true hypothesis than under its complement. This is an invented classroom scenario matching the companion script's demonstration inputs; it is not a claim about any real contract, and the numbers 0.20, 0.80, and 0.10 were chosen for illustration, not estimated from data. Notice also what happens at a boundary: if \(P(B\mid A)=0\) and \(P(B\mid A^c)=0\), the evidence has zero probability under every hypothesis in the model, and the update is undefined (division by zero) rather than silently returning the prior — this is a modeling failure to flag, not a case to paper over with an arbitrary convention.

    2.3 Odds, and why traders often think in them¶

    The odds of an event are \(o=p/(1-p)\), with inverse \(p=o/(1+o)\). Odds are algebraically equivalent to probability but multiply nicely under Bayesian updating: dividing the two branches of Bayes' rule by each other, the posterior odds equal the prior odds times the likelihood ratio,

    \[ \frac{P(A\mid B)}{P(A^c\mid B)}=\frac{P(A)}{P(A^c)}\times\frac{P(B\mid A)}{P(B\mid A^c)}. \]

    For the worked case above, prior odds are \(0.20/0.80=0.25\), the likelihood ratio is \(0.80/0.10=8\), and posterior odds are \(0.25\times8=2.0\), which converts back to \(p=2/(1+2)=0.667\) — matching the direct calculation and providing a useful arithmetic cross-check. A probability of 0.40 has odds in favor of \(0.40/0.60=2/3\), or "2-to-3 in favor"; equivalently, the odds against are \(0.60/0.40=3/2\), or "3-to-2 against." Reading a USD 0.40 contract price as that probability requires the additional price-interpretation assumptions of Chapter 4. Converting a stated probability to odds is algebra; interpreting a price as a probability is not.

    2.4 Base rates need a stated population¶

    A base rate is the prevalence of an outcome in an explicitly defined reference population; it is meaningless without saying what that population is. "Incumbents usually win" is not a base rate until you specify: which office, which country, which time period, and which subset of incumbents (all who sought re-election, or only those who survived a primary). Changing any of those changes the number, sometimes substantially, and changing the reference population after looking at a specific race you are trying to forecast is a form of selection that can silently manufacture whatever base rate is convenient.

    Base rates interact with Bayes' rule in a way that is easy to state and easy to forget under pressure: a base rate is the prior, and it should be updated by genuinely new, discriminating evidence — not by evidence that merely restates the base rate in different words. A forecaster who reads five news articles that all report the same underlying poll and updates five times because there were "five pieces of evidence" is double-, triple-, and quadruple-counting a single observation. This is the same dependence problem raised again more forcefully in Section 2.5 and in Chapter 4's discussion of aggregating many correlated poll readings into a single forecast.

    2.5 Independence and dependence, with an event-contract example¶

    Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent exactly when \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\), equivalently \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\): learning \(B\) tells you nothing new about \(A\). This is a strong, specific, checkable condition, not a default assumption to reach for out of convenience.

    Event contracts frequently violate independence in ways that are easy to miss because the contracts look unrelated on the surface. Consider two contracts: "does the incumbent party win the presidency" and "does the incumbent party retain control of the legislature," observed in the same election cycle. Both outcomes are driven substantially by shared factors — the state of the economy, the incumbent's approval rating, national mood — so learning that one resolved YES makes the other's YES resolution more likely than its unconditional probability alone would suggest. Treating these two contracts as independent when sizing a combined position understates the true joint risk: both can go wrong, or right, together far more often than the product of their individual probabilities implies. The glossary's "dependence group" concept flags sets of observations or contracts that share this kind of common exposure, so a downstream calculation does not treat them as independent draws.

    A related and even more common failure is treating repeated snapshots of the same contract, taken minutes or hours apart, as independent observations. They share the same eventual settlement; a sequence of ten price quotes leading up to one binary outcome is not ten independent bets on that outcome, no matter how many rows it occupies in a spreadsheet. This exact issue, applied to poll readings rather than price quotes, is the central empirical caution in Chapter 4's discussion of comparing prediction-market forecasts against many correlated polls from a small number of elections.

    2.6 Quantifying probability uncertainty: proper scores¶

    A probability forecast is only useful if there is a disciplined way to say whether it was good. This book uses two scoring rules for a binary outcome, both defined precisely so every chapter uses the same convention.

    The general concept behind both is a proper scoring rule: a rule for scoring a probability forecast against a realized outcome such that, in expectation, a forecaster who reports their true believed distribution cannot do better by reporting anything else. Gneiting and Raftery's inspected treatment states this as a theorem — reporting the generating distribution optimizes the expected score, with strict propriety making that optimum unique relative to the stated class of forecasts — and confirms that both the categorical quadratic (Brier) score and the logarithmic score are strictly proper. GNEITING2007 This is a statement about the scoring objective's incentive properties, not a claim about trading profit; a strictly proper score rewards honesty about probability, it does not reward a correct guess about a single coin flip.

    For one YES-probability forecast \(\hat p_i\) per binary observation \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\), the Brier score is the mean squared error of the YES probability against the realized binary label:

    \[ \operatorname{BS}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(\hat p_i-y_i)^2. \]

    This is the single-YES convention. Gneiting and Raftery's inspected categorical treatment sums the squared error over both outcomes, which is exactly twice this book's single-YES value once the reward-versus-loss sign convention is reversed; a Brier score quoted elsewhere is not automatically comparable to this book's numbers without checking which convention was used. GNEITING2007

    Log loss uses the natural logarithm of the probability the forecast assigned to whatever actually happened:

    \[ \operatorname{LL}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\Big[y_i\log\hat p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\Big]. \]

    A forecast of exactly zero on an outcome that occurs contributes an infinite loss; this is an intended property of the scoring rule, not a numerical bug to be quietly clipped away. A forecast that is exactly correct with full confidence contributes zero, using the limiting convention that \(0\log 0=0\).

    Worked case. Take four invented binary observations with labels \(y=(0,1,0,1)\) and forecasts \(\hat p=(0.1,0.7,0.4,0.8)\) — the same small example used by the companion script, so its printed output can be checked directly against this arithmetic. The squared errors are \((0.1-0)^2=0.01\), \((0.7-1)^2=0.09\), \((0.4-0)^2=0.16\), and \((0.8-1)^2=0.04\); their mean is

    \[ \operatorname{BS}=\frac{0.01+0.09+0.16+0.04}{4}=\frac{0.30}{4}=0.075. \]

    For log loss, the probability assigned to the realized outcome in each row is \(0.9,\,0.7,\,0.6,\,0.8\) respectively (using \(1-\hat p\) where \(y=0\)). Their natural logs are approximately \(-0.1054,\,-0.3567,\,-0.5108,\,-0.2231\), summing to about \(-1.1960\); dividing by \(n=4\) and negating gives

    \[ \operatorname{LL}\approx0.2990. \]

    Two comparison cases sharpen the infinite-loss boundary: a forecaster who is correctly certain, with \(y=(0,1)\) and \(\hat p=(0,1)\), incurs log loss exactly \(0\); a forecaster who is confidently wrong, with \(y=(1)\) and \(\hat p=(0)\), incurs log loss of \(+\infty\). Log loss is far harsher on confident wrongness than the Brier score, whose worst case for a single observation is bounded at \(1\). Choosing between the two, or reporting both, is developed further in Chapter 10.

    2.7 Assumptions, counterexamples, and failure modes¶

    Assumption: the information set is stated. Every probability in this book is conditional on some stated information set \(\mathcal I\); "the true probability" without reference to an information set is not a meaningful object. Two honest forecasters with different information can correctly hold different probabilities for the same event.

    Assumption: likelihoods are known or estimable. Bayes' rule is only as good as \(P(B\mid A)\) and \(P(B\mid A^c)\). In practice these are themselves estimates with their own uncertainty; treating a posterior as exact when its inputs were rough guesses overstates how much you actually know. The registry's \(p_L\) symbol — a deliberately conservative probability bound, whose inference method must be stated — keeps this humility explicit.

    Counterexample to naive independence. Two contracts on the same underlying race, phrased with different wording ("candidate wins the popular vote" and "candidate wins the electoral college"), are correlated through shared information, not independent just because they are formally different contracts with different titles.

    Failure mode: treating price movement as new evidence when it reflects old evidence being repeated. If ten outlets all cover the same single poll release, a Bayesian updater that treats each article as independent confirming evidence will overshoot its posterior. The base-rate discipline of Section 2.4 and the dependence discussion of Section 2.5 are the guardrails against exactly this.

    Failure mode: silently clipping probabilities to avoid infinite log loss. If a fitting procedure needs numerical stabilization (for example, nudging a predicted 0 to \(10^{-6}\) to avoid a crash), that stabilization must be disclosed as part of the model, not retroactively applied to change how an already-issued forecast is scored.

    2.8 Companion example¶

    The companion script bayes_scoring.py runs the Bayes update and the scoring computations worked through above, using the same numbers (prior 0.2, likelihood-if-yes 0.8, likelihood-if-no 0.1; labels \((0,1,0,1)\) against forecasts \((0.1,0.7,0.4,0.8)\)), and demonstrates the rejected-update case when the observed evidence has zero probability under the stated model. It also reports calibration bins, grouping forecasts into equal-width probability ranges and comparing each bin's mean forecast to its observed outcome rate — a first step toward the fuller calibration treatment in Chapter 10. Run it after installing the package:

    .\.venv\Scripts\python.exe examples\bayes_scoring.py

    Compare its printed posterior, Brier score, and log loss against Sections 2.2 and 2.6 above; they are the same worked numbers by construction, which is a useful way to build trust in both the hand derivation and the code.

    Exercises¶

    Exercise 2.1. Derive the posterior odds form of Bayes' rule directly from the two conditional-probability definitions used in Section 2.2, without going through the total-probability expansion. Then use it to recompute the worked case's posterior from prior odds and a likelihood ratio, and convert the result back to a probability.

    Solution sketch. Divide \(P(A\mid B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)/P(B)\) by the analogous expression for \(P(A^c\mid B)=P(B\mid A^c)P(A^c)/P(B)\); the shared denominator \(P(B)\) cancels, giving posterior odds equal to prior odds times the likelihood ratio. With prior odds \(0.20/0.80=0.25\) and likelihood ratio \(0.80/0.10=8\), posterior odds are \(2.0\), giving \(p=2/(1+2)\approx0.667\), matching Section 2.2's direct computation.

    Exercise 2.2. A base rate is quoted as "70% of contracts of this type resolve YES," drawn from a reference population of 40 historical contracts. You are told, after the fact, that 15 of those 40 came from a single recurring weekly series sharing one underlying process. Explain why this base rate may overstate the effective evidence behind it, using the dependence-group concept from Section 2.5.

    Solution sketch. If 15 of the 40 observations are draws from one recurring, correlated process rather than 15 independent trials, the true number of effectively independent data points is smaller than 40; a base rate computed by simple counting treats all 40 as equally informative, overstating precision and potentially anchoring on whatever idiosyncratic run of outcomes that one series happened to produce. A defensible estimate would either model the within-series correlation explicitly or report the base rate separately by series alongside an honest, smaller effective sample size.

    Exercise 2.3. Using the four-observation example from Section 2.6, suppose a fifth forecast is added: \(\hat p_5=0.0\) for an observation where \(y_5=1\). Recompute the Brier score and explain what happens to the log loss, without performing a numerical clipping workaround.

    Solution sketch. The Brier score becomes \(\frac{0.01+0.09+0.16+0.04+1.00}{5}=\frac{1.30}{5}=0.26\): a finite, if much larger, number, because the squared error contribution from a maximally wrong confident forecast is bounded at 1. Log loss, by contrast, becomes \(+\infty\), because the fifth term is \(-\log(0)\), which diverges; the correct response is to report and investigate this infinite loss (it flags a forecaster who was certain and wrong), not to substitute a small nonzero probability after the fact to make the number finite.

    Exercise 2.4. Explain, using the odds form of Bayes' rule, why evidence that is equally likely under a hypothesis and its complement cannot move a Bayesian forecaster's probability, however emotionally compelling that evidence feels.

    Solution sketch. If \(P(B\mid A)=P(B\mid A^c)\), the likelihood ratio is exactly 1, so posterior odds equal prior odds, and therefore posterior probability equals prior probability; the evidence carries zero Bayesian information regardless of how vivid or persuasive it seems, because "informativeness" here is defined strictly by how differently the evidence's probability depends on the hypothesis, not by its emotional salience.

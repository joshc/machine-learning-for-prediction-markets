    5. Order books and automated market makers¶

    Prerequisites¶

    Chapters 1-4, especially the executable-price discipline of Chapter 3 and the price-interpretation cautions of Chapter 4. Comfort with exponentials, logarithms, and basic algebra. One optional differentiation step connects a cost function to its marginal prices; the loss bound itself uses only elementary inequalities.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to: describe how a real central limit order book (CLOB) represents depth, queue priority, and order types, and why that representation differs from a textbook single "price"; derive the logarithmic market scoring rule's (LMSR) cost function behavior and its bounded-loss property from first principles, given the liquidity parameter \(B\); compute LMSR prices and trade costs for a small worked example and explain what the parameter \(B\) controls economically; and state clearly why LMSR is a mechanism-design construction, not a description of how either Kalshi or Polymarket's live order books work.

    5.1 Two different objects called "a market"¶

    This book has so far treated "the market" as a source of an executable ask and bid. That is a black-box view, sufficient for the decision arithmetic in Chapter 3, but it hides two structurally different mechanisms that produce those prices in real venues, and understanding both matters once you start reasoning about depth, price impact, and what a "thin" market really means.

    A central limit order book (CLOB) is a list of resting orders on each side of the market, sorted by price and, within a price level, typically by arrival time. Kalshi's documented orderbook response schema returns arrays of bid levels for each side, with a stated dollar price and a fixed-point quantity at each level, and explains that opposite-outcome bids imply the corresponding asks under ordinary binary mechanics. KALSHI_BOOK Polymarket's documented public book interface similarly identifies a specific outcome token and returns price and size levels alongside market constraints and book-state metadata. POLYMARKET_BOOK In a CLOB, prices and depth emerge from whatever independent participants have chosen to post; there is no single formula generating them, and a large buy order sweeps through however much resting liquidity happens to be posted, exactly as Chapter 3's depth-limited sweep-cost example showed.

    An automated market maker (AMM), by contrast, is a specified mathematical mechanism that always quotes a price as a function of its own current inventory or state, without requiring an opposing human order to be resting at that moment. The logarithmic market scoring rule, the subject of the rest of this chapter, is one influential AMM design among others, and it is not how either venue examined in this book's documentation operates its central markets — worth stating plainly before working through the mechanism, so the mathematics below is never mistaken for a description of Kalshi's or Polymarket's live books.

    5.2 Real CLOB mechanics: depth, queues, and order types¶

    A CLOB's practical behavior depends on several features a single "current price" number does not capture.

    Depth is the quantity available at each price level away from the best bid and ask. As Chapter 3 demonstrated, an order whose size exceeds the best level's quantity sweeps into worse prices, and the realized average price depends on the entire depth profile, not just the touch price. A market can have a tight bid-ask spread and still be effectively illiquid for any order beyond a small size if the depth behind the touch is thin.

    Queue priority determines which resting order at a given price level executes first when an opposing order arrives. Common conventions prioritize orders by arrival time within a price level, meaning two resting orders at the identical price can have very different chances of filling depending purely on when they were placed. A backtest that does not model queue position, and simply assumes its own hypothetical resting order fills whenever the touch price crosses it, can substantially overstate historical fill rates; the glossary's "queue priority" and "order-book replay" entries flag exactly this risk.

    Order types commonly include market orders (execute immediately against the best available opposing depth, accepting whatever price results), limit orders (execute only at a specified price or better, controlling acceptable price but not whether or when a fill occurs), and various conditional or time-in-force variants. A limit order sitting unfilled is not "no position"; it can represent reserved cash (Chapter 3's worst-case reservation logic) with no guarantee of ever executing, and a canceled limit order that never filled produced no cash flow at all despite having been live in the book for some period.

    Fixed-point and rounding conventions matter more than they might seem. Kalshi's documented order-book schema represents prices and fixed-point quantities as decimal strings; quantities must not be assumed to be whole integers. Its separate fee-rounding documentation describes rounding behavior accumulated across the fills of a single order rather than applied independently per unit. KALSHI_BOOK KALSHI_ROUNDING A classroom flat per-unit fee, as used in Chapters 1 and 3, is a simplification precisely because it ignores this kind of rounding-accumulator behavior; Chapter 6 returns to the fee-rounding documentation in more depth.

    5.3 The LMSR cost function, derived¶

    The logarithmic market scoring rule is a mechanism for a specified automated market maker to always quote a price for each of \(n\) mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes, parameterized by a single liquidity parameter \(B\) (written with a capital letter throughout this book specifically to avoid confusion with the executable bid \(b\)). Hanson's foundational treatment develops market scoring rules as a way to combine individual elicitation with group information aggregation, with the logarithmic form carrying particular conditional-probability preservation properties; this book did not independently inspect the full proof of those properties and presents the derivation below as an original, self-contained classroom construction rather than a reproduction of the source's proof. HANSON2007

    Let \(q=(q_1,\dots,q_n)\) denote the market maker's current cumulative position in each outcome (how many shares of each outcome have net been sold by the mechanism so far). Assume \(B>0\) and normalize each winning share's payout to one unit of account, so \(C\) is a cost in that unit. Define the cost function

    \[ C(q)=B\,\log\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n e^{q_i/B}\right). \]

    The price of outcome \(i\) at state \(q\) is the partial derivative of \(C\) with respect to \(q_i\), which works out (by direct differentiation of the log-sum-exp expression) to the softmax function

    \[ \pi_i(q)=\frac{e^{q_i/B}}{\sum_{j=1}^n e^{q_j/B}}. \]

    These marginal prices are positive and sum to one by construction. That normalization is consistent with the mutually exclusive, exhaustive payout structure assumed here, but it cannot establish that the actual contracts have that structure. The designer still has to verify the outcome space; a softmax over incompatible contracts would normalize prices for the wrong payoff model.

    The cost of a trade moving the position from \(q\) to \(q+\delta\) is simply \(C(q+\delta)-C(q)\): the mechanism charges exactly the change in its own cost function, which is why \(C\) is called a cost function rather than merely a pricing function.

    5.4 Why the cost function is numerically stable, and why that matters¶

    A direct computation of \(\sum_i e^{q_i/B}\) can overflow for large \(q_i/B\), since exponentials grow extremely fast. The standard remedy, used in the companion implementation, is to factor out the largest term before summing: writing \(q_{\max}=\max_i q_i\),

    \[ C(q)=q_{\max}+B\log\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n e^{(q_i-q_{\max})/B}\right), \]

    which follows by factoring out \(e^{q_{\max}/B}\) and using \(B\log(e^{q_{\max}/B}S)=q_{\max}+B\log S\). Each remaining exponent is nonpositive; at least one exponential is exactly one, and the sum lies between one and \(n\). This avoids overflow in the exponentials. It does not remove every floating-point limitation: extremely large inputs or subtracting two nearly equal large costs can still lose precision. The companion's large-position example checks a particular finite calculation, not arbitrary numerical magnitudes.

    5.5 A worked numerical case: buying into an LMSR market¶

    Take a two-outcome LMSR market (YES/NO) with liquidity parameter \(B=10\), starting from the neutral state \(q=(0,0)\). Initial prices are \(\pi=(0.5,0.5)\) by symmetry, matching the softmax of two equal values.

    A trader buys 10 YES shares, moving the position from \((0,0)\) to \((10,0)\). Applying the cost function directly:

    \[ C(0,0)=10\log(e^0+e^0)=10\log 2\approx6.9315, \]
    \[ C(10,0)=10\log(e^{1}+e^{0})\approx10\log(2.71828+1)=10\log(3.71828)\approx13.1326. \]

    The trade cost is the difference, \(13.1326-6.9315\approx6.2011\) dollars for 10 shares — noticeably more than the naive \(10\times0.5=5.00\) that the initial price alone would suggest, because the mechanism's price rises continuously as the position moves toward YES. The post-trade price is \(\pi_{\text{YES}}=e^{1}/(e^{1}+e^{0})\approx0.7311\): after this single trade, buying the next marginal share of YES would cost roughly 73 cents, not 50.

    Now repeat the identical trade with a larger liquidity parameter, \(B=100\). The same computation gives \(C(0,0)=100\log2\approx69.3147\) and \(C(10,0)=100\log(e^{0.1}+1)\approx74.4397\), so the cost of the same 10-share trade is only about \(5.1249\) — much closer to the naive \(5.00\) — and the post-trade price moves only to about \(0.5250\). This is the precise economic meaning of \(B\): it controls how much the mechanism's price moves in response to a given trade size, in exactly the same conceptual role that order-book depth plays in a CLOB. A larger \(B\) means a "deeper," less price-sensitive market that can absorb larger trades with smaller price impact, at the cost of the operator being willing to accept a larger worst-case loss, which is exactly the quantity derived next.

    5.6 The bounded-loss property, derived¶

    A classic and useful property of this cost function is that the market maker's worst-case loss, over all possible sequences of trades and all possible eventual winning outcomes, is bounded by \(B\log n\), independent of how much volume actually trades. Deriving this cleanly requires one elementary inequality about the log-sum-exp function: for any real vector \(x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\),

    \[ \max_i x_i \;\leq\; \log\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n e^{x_i}\right) \;\leq\; \max_i x_i + \log n. \]

    The left inequality holds because the sum of exponentials includes the single term \(e^{\max_i x_i}\) plus other nonnegative terms, so the sum is at least \(e^{\max_i x_i}\), and taking logs preserves the inequality. The right inequality holds because every term in the sum satisfies \(e^{x_i}\leq e^{\max_i x_i}\), so the sum is at most \(n\,e^{\max_i x_i}\), and taking logs gives \(\log(\text{sum})\leq \max_i x_i+\log n\).

    Apply this with \(x_i=q_i/B\), and multiply through by \(B\):

    \[ \max_i q_i \;\leq\; C(q) \;\leq\; \max_i q_i + B\log n. \]

    Now consider the mechanism's loss if outcome \(k\) is the one that eventually settles YES: every share of outcome \(k\) that was sold by the mechanism must be paid one dollar, so the mechanism's total payout obligation is \(q_k\) (the final cumulative position in the winning outcome), while the total cash the mechanism collected over all trades is \(C(q)-C(0)\), by repeated application of the cost-difference rule from Section 5.3 summed over every trade. The mechanism's loss is therefore

    \[ \text{loss}_k = q_k - \big(C(q)-C(0)\big). \]

    Because \(q_k\leq\max_i q_i\leq C(q)\) (the left half of the bound above, applied to the final state), we have \(q_k - C(q)\leq0\), so \(\text{loss}_k\leq C(0)\). And \(C(0)=B\log(\sum_i e^0)=B\log n\) exactly, since every \(q_i\) starts at zero. Therefore

    \[ \text{loss}_k \;\leq\; B\log n \quad\text{for every possible winning outcome } k, \]

    with the bound approached as the winning outcome's position exceeds every losing position by an arbitrarily large amount. Increasing all positions equally does not approach the bound because only relative positions affect prices. For two outcomes and \(B=10\), the bound is \(10\log2\approx6.93\) units of account. It bounds this cost-function mechanism's payout loss under its stated assumptions, not operational failures, changing payout rules, or an operator's unrelated liabilities. A larger \(B\) buys deeper quoted liquidity at the cost of a larger possible subsidy; the bound is not a profit guarantee.

    5.7 Assumptions, counterexamples, and failure modes¶

    Assumption: outcomes are genuinely mutually exclusive and exhaustive. The softmax-sums-to-one structure in Section 5.3 depends on this being true by construction of the outcome space fed into the mechanism. If the underlying real-world outcomes are not actually exclusive (recall Chapter 1's categorical-contract caution), an LMSR built over a mis-specified outcome set produces prices summing to one over the wrong partition — a modeling error, not a mechanism failure.

    Assumption: the operator can absorb the bounded loss. The \(B\log n\) bound is an upper limit, not a guarantee the loss is small or affordable; larger \(B\) directly increases the maximum possible loss, so choosing \(B\) trades depth against worst-case subsidy cost rather than being a free parameter to maximize.

    Counterexample: splitting an order does not remove its LMSR impact. With no intervening trades, splitting the same net purchase into pieces costs exactly the same as one purchase: \([C(q+\delta_1)-C(q)]+[C(q+\delta_1+\delta_2)-C(q+\delta_1)]=C(q+\delta_1+\delta_2)-C(q)\). The intermediate costs cancel. Waiting can change the result only if other trading changes the state (or additional fees or rules intervene); it does not mechanically make a path-independent cost function cheaper.

    Failure mode: treating LMSR as a universal description of live venues. Neither Kalshi's documented order-book schema nor Polymarket's documented public book interface describes an LMSR cost function; both describe conventional order-book structures with independently posted bid and ask levels. KALSHI_BOOK POLYMARKET_BOOK Do not import LMSR's bounded-loss or truthful-elicitation properties into either venue's actual order book without separately verifying that the venue uses this mechanism.

    Failure mode: assuming LMSR generalizes beyond two outcomes without checking exclusivity. The multi-outcome case requires the same exclusivity verification Chapter 1 demanded of any categorical contract; convex market-design work extends bounded-payoff mechanism ideas to combinatorial outcome spaces, but design feasibility is a different claim from a guarantee that any two real markets can be costlessly combined into one such structure. ABERNETHY2013

    Exercises¶

    Exercise 5.1. Using \(B=100\) (Section 5.5's second case), compute the LMSR price after a much larger trade that moves the position to \(q=(50,0)\), and compare the price impact to the \(q=(10,0)\) case.

    Solution sketch. \(\pi_{\text{YES}}=e^{0.5}/(e^{0.5}+e^{0})\approx1.6487/2.6487\approx0.6225\). Compared to the price of about 0.525 after a 10-share trade at the same \(B\), a 50-share trade moves the price noticeably further, illustrating that price impact grows with trade size even at a fixed liquidity parameter, not just as \(B\) shrinks.

    Exercise 5.2. For a three-outcome LMSR market with \(B=5\), compute the worst-case bound on the operator's loss, and explain in one sentence why adding a fourth genuinely exclusive outcome to the same market, at the same \(B\), increases that bound.

    Solution sketch. The bound is \(B\log n=5\log3\approx5.4931\). Adding a fourth outcome increases \(n\) to 4, giving \(5\log4\approx6.9315\); the bound grows with \(\log n\) because the initial cost \(C(0)=B\log n\) (the cost of committing to quote prices for a wider exclusive partition before any trading occurs) itself grows with the number of outcomes being simultaneously covered.

    Exercise 5.3. A backtest replays a historical order book by assuming a hypothetical resting limit order at the touch price always executes whenever a later trade crosses that price level. Explain, using the queue-priority concept from Section 5.2, why this assumption can overstate historical performance, and propose one concrete way to make the assumption more conservative.

    Solution sketch. Without knowing the hypothetical order's actual arrival time relative to other resting orders at the same price, the backtest cannot know its true queue position; if many other orders were already resting ahead of it, the touch price could be crossed several times over by other participants' orders before the hypothetical order would actually reach the front of the queue and fill, so assuming an immediate fill overstates the number and speed of fills the strategy would have realized. A more conservative approach would require the touch price to be crossed by some minimum multiple of the hypothetical order's own size, or require a specified time delay past the first crossing, before assuming a fill, and would report both assumptions explicitly as replay simplifications rather than as measured executions.

    Exercise 5.4. Explain why the statement "LMSR guarantees the market maker will lose at most \(B\log n\)" is a statement about mechanism design, not a statement that any real prediction-market venue examined in this book is bounded-loss, and identify the specific documentation gap that prevents extending the claim to Kalshi or Polymarket.

    Solution sketch. The \(B\log n\) bound is a mathematical property of the specific cost function \(C(q)=B\log\sum e^{q_i/B}\) derived in Section 5.6; it applies only to a mechanism that actually implements this cost function as its pricing rule. Neither Kalshi's inspected order-book documentation nor Polymarket's inspected order-book documentation describes such a cost function; both describe independently posted bid/ask levels from a central limit order book, so there is no basis in the inspected documentation for attributing an LMSR-style bounded-loss guarantee to either venue's actual market structure.

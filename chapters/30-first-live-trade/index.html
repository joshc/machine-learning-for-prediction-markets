    30. From paper evidence to a first live trade¶

    Prerequisites¶

    This is the book's final chapter, and it assumes you have worked through, or at least read, everything before it: the contract and probability foundations of Part I, the data and evaluation discipline of Part II, the modeling chapters of Part III, the execution, evidence, risk, and paper-operations chapters of Part IV, the research-hypothesis discipline of Chapter 25, and all four preceding quickstarts. You need the canonical accounting example from the notation registry, the evidence-gate concept introduced in Chapter 26, and a clear memory of every NO_TRADE and every honestly reported loss in Chapters 27 through 29.

    Learning objectives¶

    By the end of this chapter you should be able to run the first-trade checklist script and correctly interpret its DO_NOT_PROCEED_FROM_SYNTHETIC_EVIDENCE status, reproduce the book's canonical cost, profit, and expected-value accounting from memory, work a first-dollar illustrative sizing exercise while keeping expected profit and realized profit conceptually separate, explain in detail what each item on the manual checklist actually requires a reader to do, and state precisely why no configuration of the educational companion's evidence flags can automatically authorize a live trade. This chapter does not introduce new theory. It concludes the book by turning everything already developed into one guarded, still-manual decision.

    30.1 What this chapter is, and deliberately is not¶

    Every preceding quickstart ended in an honest NO_TRADE, a marked loss, or a conditional candidate that depended on assumptions the script could not itself verify. That pattern was not an accident of which examples happened to get written. It reflects the evidence supplied by the educational companion: every default script is synthetic, illustrative, or, in the NOAA case, a real but current-vintage forecasting exercise with no attached market, price, or fee. None of it, individually or combined, constitutes verified evidence that a specific real trade, on a specific real venue, at a specific real price, would have a positive expected value net of actual costs.

    This chapter therefore does not attempt to supply the missing piece with a cleverer model, a bigger dataset, or a new theoretical result. It instead does the only honest thing left to do: lay out, in concrete and checkable detail, what a reader would need to independently verify before placing a first real, funded, cash-settled trade, using the book's own canonical accounting to make the arithmetic of that decision precise. The book ends here, with a checklist and a decision left to a human, not with an appendix promising a shortcut around it.

    30.2 Running the checklist and reading its output¶

    From the companion project root (book\companion), after the setup described in Chapter 26:

    Command working directory: run this chapter's Python commands from book\companion. From the trading repository root, run Set-Location book\companion once first. The .venv belongs to that companion project; see companion setup.

    .\.venv\Scripts\python.exe examples\first_trade_checklist.py

    Open examples/first_trade_checklist.py while reading; its module docstring states that it cannot place a funded order. The following is an excerpt of the observed output, with some wrapper and explanatory fields omitted:

    status is the literal string DO_NOT_PROCEED_FROM_SYNTHETIC_EVIDENCE. This is not a placeholder waiting for a future software update; it is the correct and permanent answer for any run of this script against its own bundled fixtures, because those fixtures are, by construction, synthetic. invented_accounting_only reflects the same canonical_position() helper used throughout the companion: ten YES units bought at a 0.40 ask with the fixture's flat USD 0.01 fee, for an open cost basis of 4.10, leaving cash of 5.90 from an initial 10.00. maximum_loss_if_this_binary_position_settles_NO is exactly that cost basis, 4.10: if this position resolves NO, the entire 4.10 already paid is lost, and nothing further is owed beyond it, because this is a fully collateralized, long-only binary position with no borrowing.

    30.3 The canonical accounting this whole book has used¶

    It is worth restating the book's one canonical worked example here, at the end, exactly as the notation registry fixes it, because every risk and sizing decision in this final chapter reduces to the same four numbers. Buying 10 YES units at an ask of USD 0.40, with the book's invented flat USD 0.01 per-unit fee, costs

    \[ 10 \times (0.40 + 0.01) = 4.10. \]

    Under ordinary binary settlement this position pays USD 10 if YES and USD 0 if NO, for a net profit of USD 5.90 or a net loss of USD 4.10. If a model assigns this contract a probability of 0.55, the modeled expected net profit is

    \[ 10 \times (0.55 - 0.40 - 0.01) = 1.40. \]

    This 1.40 figure is a model-conditional expectation, computed under a forecast that might simply be wrong; it is not evidence that the position has a real edge, and it says nothing about what the very next resolution of this contract will actually produce. If the position is instead closed early by selling all ten units at a bid of USD 0.46, with a further USD 0.01-per-unit exit fee, the realized round-trip profit is

    \[ 10 \times (0.46 - 0.40 - 0.01 - 0.01) = 0.40. \]

    Keep these four numbers, 4.10 cost, 5.90 or minus-4.10 at settlement, 1.40 modeled expected value at \(\hat p = 0.55\), and 0.40 realized on an early resale, distinct in your own head before reading further, because Section 30.5's checklist depends on a reader being able to keep exactly this kind of distinction straight under the pressure of an actual decision.

    30.4 A first-dollar illustrative sizing exercise¶

    Here is a deliberately narrow, purely deterministic accounting exercise, separate from the modeled expected-value calculation above, and it is worth being precise about which question each one answers. Suppose a reader wants to know: at the same 0.40 ask and USD 0.01 per-unit fee, what is the smallest whole number of units that would produce at least USD 1.00 of net profit if the position resolves YES? This is a conditional, deterministic accounting question. It is not an expected-value calculation, and it requires no probability estimate at all.

    Per-unit cost is \(0.40+0.01=0.41\); per-unit profit if YES is \(1.00-0.41=0.59\). Solving \(Q \times 0.59 \geq 1.00\) gives \(Q \geq 1.6949\ldots\), so the smallest whole unit quantity is \(Q=2\). At \(Q=2\), cost is \(2\times0.41=0.82\), well within the 5.90 of available cash left in the canonical position's broker after its first ten-unit fill, and the planned profit if YES is \(2\times0.59=1.18\). The maximum loss on this same two-unit lot, if it instead resolves NO, is the full 0.82 cost basis.

    State plainly what this exercise does and does not establish. It answers "how many units, at this price and fee, would be needed to reach a round number of profit conditional on winning." It does not answer "should I expect to win," which requires an actual probability estimate and produces the different, expected-value quantity computed in Section 30.3. It also does not promise that USD 1.18 will ever be realized: this is a target computed under a hypothetical favorable outcome, not a guarantee, and a binary contract that resolves NO instead loses the full 0.82, regardless of how carefully the quantity was chosen to hit a round profit target on the other branch. Confusing this deterministic "if YES" accounting with either a promised or an expected outcome is exactly the kind of error this book has repeatedly warned against, and it is worth naming explicitly here, in the chapter where a reader is most likely to be tempted to skip past the distinction under real time pressure.

    30.5 Working through each guarded manual check¶

    The checklist script's six items are deliberately terse in its own JSON output. Here is what each one actually requires a reader to do, in detail, not merely to acknowledge.

    Eligibility, jurisdiction, and current venue terms independently checked. Visit the venue's own current terms and eligibility pages yourself, at the time you are actually trading, rather than relying on this book's research notes, which are dated to one audit day and explicitly could not establish a complete eligibility or reuse-permission picture. The inspected Polymarket terms response, for example, displayed a geographic/product restriction notice without exposing its complete operative terms. POLYMARKET_TERMS Never attempt a VPN, IP masking, or any other bypass of a displayed restriction; an unclear terms page is a reason to stop, not a problem to route around.

    Exact contract rules, exceptional payouts, and collateral understood. Read the specific contract's own rules document, not a general description of "how this kind of market usually works." Identify whether an exceptional settlement, dispute process, or partial payout is possible, and in what collateral; Polymarket's documented dispute and 50/50 payout process, and its separate pUSD-versus-USDC collateral distinction, are concrete examples of detail a short title cannot convey. POLYMARKET_RESOLUTION POLYMARKET_COLLATERAL

    Real point-in-time evidence independently reviewed after strategy search. Any forecasting claim behind a real trade needs evaluation on data genuinely available at decision time, with every trial and adjustment logged, in the sense of Chapter 19, not merely "backtested well once it looked good." Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado, and Zhu's backtest-overfitting framework is the relevant qualified reminder that repeated holdout inspection during research is itself a mechanism for producing an impressive but unreliable result. BAILEY2017

    Current fees, rounding, executable depth, and maximum affordable loss checked. Re-verify the venue's actual, currently effective fee schedule immediately before trading, since documented fee tables and rounding rules are explicitly time-varying. KALSHI_ROUNDING POLYMARKET_FEES Confirm executable depth at the price you intend to pay, not a stale quote, and compute the maximum possible loss using the same cost-basis arithmetic as Section 30.3 before doing anything else.

    Open inventory, reserved cash, and correlated loss reviewed. Account for existing positions' combined loss under a joint adverse scenario, not each in isolation; Chapter 22's bankroll discussion and this book's scenario_stress helper both exist because individually small positions can produce an unacceptable combined loss under correlated events.

    A written manual decision, rejection conditions and outcome journal prepared. Before placing any order, write down why you believe the trade is justified, the conditions under which you would consider yourself wrong and exit, and a place to honestly record the eventual outcome. This is not a bureaucratic formality; it is the defense against rationalizing a bad decision, or forgetting how many similar decisions were actually made and how they turned out.

    30.6 Why the evidence gate cannot be toggled into a live pass¶

    Read prediction_market_lab.evidence.assess_evidence directly. It accepts six explicit boolean fields, point_in_time_verified, execution_history_verified, costs_verified, untouched_evaluation, dependence_and_search_accounted_for, and uncertainty_supports_positive_net_result, plus a synthetic flag and a nonempty provenance tuple, and it raises ValueError outright if any of these is not a genuine boolean. Suppose, hypothetically, a reader constructed an Evidence object with synthetic=False, real provenance strings, and every one of those six booleans set to True, representing a claim that every one of these six conditions had actually been satisfied by real, independently reviewed work. Even in that best possible case, assess_evidence does not return anything resembling a "cleared to trade live" status. It returns RESEARCH_REVIEW_REQUIRED, with the explicit accompanying reason "declarations require independent audit; no automatic live promotion".

    There is no flag that converts a paper decision into a funded one. Complete declarations can request independent research review, not perform that review or authorize an order. synthetic_evidence() sets synthetic=True and point_in_time_verified=True for the fixture's explicitly constructed teaching clocks; its remaining five verification flags are false. This does not certify real historical availability: the separate synthetic flag itself blocks empirical live-edge inference. Every quickstart therefore returns REJECT_LIVE_EDGE_INFERENCE, describing its actual evidence rather than imposing an arbitrary penalty.

    30.7 There is no paper-to-live unlock, and this chapter does not invent one¶

    Nothing in the book's educational companion stores an API key, signs a transaction, submits a funded order, or contains a hidden or documented switch that promotes a paper decision, however favorable, into a live one. This boundary applies to book\companion, not to separate trading-system code elsewhere in the repository. The market_data module, documented in data/README.md and the examples README, performs only optional, explicitly acknowledged (--acknowledge-terms) read-only public metadata and order-book snapshot requests against four allowlisted endpoints; it has no coupling to PaperBroker, no order-submission capability, and no credential handling, and it is neither a live broker nor a historical archive. No default example calls it, the companion ships no acquired venue payload, and its output is a separate, locally gitignored file a reader must explicitly request. A separately documented mock-funds demo and a documented live/historical data split at one venue are likewise not a claim of live-market execution fidelity or a complete historical order-book archive; treat any paper, demo, or historical-replay result as rehearsal, not proof. KALSHI_DEMO KALSHI_HISTORY A first real trade, if a reader independently decides to make one after completing every check in Section 30.5, happens entirely outside the educational companion, on a venue's own interface, under its own account, terms, and current operative rules. This book supplies the accounting discipline, the failure vocabulary, and the checklist; it does not, and by design cannot, supply the trade itself.

    30.8 Troubleshooting¶

    If you find yourself editing evidence.py or the checklist script to force a different status, stop: that would not create real verified evidence, only relabel the exact same synthetic fixtures this chapter has already accounted for. If you are unsure whether a real check genuinely satisfies one of Section 30.5's six items, hold the corresponding flag at False rather than rounding it up; an explicit boolean should describe only what has actually been verified. If your written decision record from Section 30.5's sixth check feels uncomfortable to write honestly, that discomfort is informative and should not be resolved by skipping the exercise.

    30.9 Exercises and solution sketches¶

    Exercise 1: recomputing the canonical numbers from memory¶

    Without looking back at Section 30.3, write down the cost, the two settlement outcomes, the modeled expected profit at \(\hat p = 0.55\), and the realized resale profit for the book's canonical ten-unit example, then check your answer.

    Solution sketch. Cost is \(10\times(0.40+0.01)=4.10\); settlement outcomes are \(+5.90\) on YES and \(-4.10\) on NO; modeled expected profit at \(\hat p=0.55\) is \(10\times(0.55-0.41)=1.40\); realized resale profit at a bid of 0.46 with a further 0.01 exit fee is \(10\times(0.46-0.40-0.01-0.01)=0.40\).

    Exercise 2: a first-dollar target at a different price¶

    Using the same USD 0.01 per-unit fee, find the smallest whole-unit quantity that produces at least USD 2.00 of profit if the position resolves YES, at an ask of 0.55 instead of 0.40.

    Solution sketch. Per-unit cost is \(0.55+0.01=0.56\); per-unit profit if YES is \(1.00-0.56=0.44\). Solving \(Q\times0.44\geq2.00\) gives \(Q\geq4.545\ldots\), so the smallest whole quantity is \(Q=5\), costing \(5\times0.56=2.80\) and producing a planned if-YES profit of \(5\times0.44=2.20\), with a maximum loss of 2.80 if the position instead resolves NO.

    Exercise 3: why RESEARCH_REVIEW_REQUIRED is not a green light¶

    A reader argues that since RESEARCH_REVIEW_REQUIRED is a materially different, less severe status than REJECT_LIVE_EDGE_INFERENCE, reaching it should be treated as sufficient grounds to place a live trade. Explain what is wrong with this argument.

    Solution sketch. RESEARCH_REVIEW_REQUIRED means only that the declared evidence conditions, if honestly true, would justify independent human research review; its own accompanying reason states explicitly that it requires independent audit and grants no automatic live promotion. It is a description of what evidence would be worth someone's further scrutiny, not a verdict that the underlying claims have actually been audited, or that they are correct, or that the resulting trading decision is safe. Treating it as a green light substitutes a code path's internal label for the actual human review that label is asking for.

    Exercise 4: a maximum-loss check across two correlated positions¶

    A reader already holds the canonical ten-unit position from Section 30.2, with a maximum loss of 4.10, and is considering the two-unit position from Section 30.4, with a maximum loss of 0.82, where both contracts' resolutions are known to be strongly positively correlated. State the appropriate combined maximum-loss figure to use when deciding whether this fits an affordable-loss limit, and explain why simply adding the two average losses would understate the relevant risk.

    Solution sketch. Under strong positive correlation, the appropriate planning figure is the sum of both positions' maximum losses under the shared adverse scenario in which both resolve NO together, \(4.10+0.82=4.92\), not some smaller blended or averaged figure; positive correlation means the bad outcomes tend to occur together rather than offsetting one another, so the relevant risk-budget comparison is against this joint worst case, exactly the discipline scenario_stress and Chapter 22's bankroll material both insist on.

    Closing the book¶

    This book began by insisting that an event contract is not a stock, that a probability is not an oracle, and that a favorable-looking number requires evidence before it earns trust. Twenty-nine chapters and four prior practical quickstarts later, ending in this fifth and final one, that discipline has not loosened; if anything, this closing chapter has tightened it, by refusing to let even a fully truthful set of evidence declarations translate into an automatic live-trading permission. Every default example in the educational companion is synthetic, illustrative, and offline for a reason: so a reader can build every habit this book teaches, calibration discipline, cost-aware decision screens, honest no-trade outcomes, and risk budgets that bind even against a positive expected value, entirely without financial risk, before ever bringing a genuinely real dollar into contact with a genuinely real market. When that moment eventually comes, if it does, it belongs to the reader, working through Section 30.5's checks with their own current, independently verified information, not to any script in this book.
